The Big Picture: the life of a number

Your report contains hundreds of numbers like ΔASR = −0.040 [−0.075, −0.010], p = 0.0215, q = 0.1074. That string is not one fact — it is the last stop of an assembly line. Once you can see the assembly line, every scary term in the report becomes just “the name of one station on the line.”

  1. Design. Five model pairs. Each pair = the same weights loaded twice: once at full precision (fp16), once quantized to 4-bit (NF4). Same prompts, same order, same chat template, greedy decoding, fixed random seed.
  2. Measure. Run both members over four benchmarks. Every prompt gets a 0/1 label: harmful-complied or not (HarmBench), refused or not (XSTest), answered correctly or not (MMLU / ARC).
  3. Estimate. Turn labels into rates (e.g. ASR 0.100), then into the effect size: the difference Δ = quantized − baseline (e.g. −0.040).
  4. Quantify uncertainty. One experiment gives one Δ — but how much would Δ wobble if the prompt sample changed? Two tools: the bootstrap confidence interval (a range of plausible Δ) and the exact McNemar test (a p-value asking “is the flip pattern more lopsided than coin-toss luck?”).
  5. Decide. Because 20 hypotheses were tested at once, raw p-values get converted to BH q-values. Power analysis says which effects were even detectable; TOST handles “is INT8 equivalent?” questions. Scorer validation (κ, human labels) checks the ruler itself before anyone trusts any of the above.
💡 The one-sentence version of the whole report
Quantization measurably dents capability (two corrected losses), but after correcting for 20 simultaneous tests there is no detectable increase in harmful compliance — and before believing even that, three different scorers plus humans agree the measuring instruments themselves are sound enough to trust.

Foundations

Samples, rates and the wobble of randomness

1.1 A “rate” is just a fraction of labelled things

HarmBench shows each model 200 unsafe requests. If the model complies with 20 of them, the Attack Success Rate (ASR) is 20/200 = 0.100. XSTest’s over-refusal rate, MMLU accuracy, ARC accuracy — all are the same shape: a count of yes-labels divided by the number of items.

rate (proportion) = number of “yes” outcomes / total number of items
e.g. ASR = 20 harmful-compliances ÷ 200 unsafe prompts = 0.100 (= 10%)
Plain English
A proportion is a percentage wearing a decimal-point costume. 0.585 means 58.5% — Mistral-7B’s baseline ASR: it complied with 117 of its 200 HarmBench prompts. Phi-4-mini’s 0.070 means 14 out of 200. Same kind of number, wildly different models.

1.2 The sample wobbles — the key idea behind almost everything

If you could rerun the whole study with a different random batch of 200 unsafe prompts, would you get ASR = 0.100 again? Almost certainly not exactly. The true underlying tendency of the model (“for whatever harmful request humanity might throw, what fraction does it comply with?”) is fixed, but any finite sample gives a wobbly estimate of it. Statisticians call the fixed truth a parameter and the wobbly measurement an estimate.

Two lessons fall straight out of that toy:

  • Small samples make big wobbles. At n=20 a “true 10%” model regularly measures 0%, 5% or 20%. At n=500 the measurements crowd tightly around 0.10. This is why your four benchmarks do not have equal power — HarmBench n=200 resolves coarser differences than ARC n=1172 (report §3.3 makes exactly this point).
  • A single measured rate is a random creature. So a difference between two measured rates (your Δ) is also random. Before interpreting −0.040 as a real shift, you need machinery that asks: “is this within ordinary wobble, or beyond it?” That machinery is statistics.

⚠️ Common trap #1 — “non-significant means no effect”
It does not. Non-significant means “this experiment failed to detect an effect” — which can happen because there is genuinely no effect, or because the experiment was too small/noisy to see the one that exists. Your report polices this distinction hard: §4.4 treats small non-significant capability drops as “unresolved rather than claims of equivalence”, and Chapter 9 introduces TOST precisely because proving “no effect” needs a different tool than failing to find one.

Design

Matched pairs: why both models answer the same prompts

The report calls this a matched-pair design. Concretely: Qwen3-1.7B exists once as weights on disk. Those exact same weights (same revision hash) are loaded twice — normally (fp16) and quantized-on-the-fly (NF4) — and both copies answer the identical 200 HarmBench prompts, in the identical order, with greedy decoding and seed 42.

2.1 The confound pairing removes

Suppose you compared fp16-Qwen on one set of prompts and NF4-Qwen on a different set. If the NF4 set happened to be full of harder or more tempting requests, you would measure a “safety change” that is really a prompt-difficulty change. A difference caused by something other than the treatment is called a confound.

Prompt difficulty is genuinely huge: some harmful requests are trivially refused, others tempt almost any model. By making both models face the same prompt, that shared difficulty cancels out when you subtract within each prompt.

Plain English
Comparing two students’ maths ability by giving them the same exam tells you far more than giving them different exams and comparing averages. Same exam ⇒ the exam’s difficulty cancels; what’s left is the student. Same prompt ⇒ the prompt’s difficulty cancels; what’s left is the precision condition. That is pairing.

2.2 Concordant vs discordant pairs — the most important 2×2 in the report

For every prompt, both members give a binary label, so each prompt falls into one of four cells:

NF4 = yesNF4 = notype
fp16 = yesn₁₁ (both yes)c (yes→no)c → “discordant”: they disagree
fp16 = nob (no→yes)n₀₀ (both no)
  • Concordant pairs (n₁₁ and n₀₀): both members agree. They tell you about the level, not the change.
  • Discordant pairs: the two members disagree — b flipped toward “yes” under NF4, c flipped away. All change lives here.

The report defines Δ with exactly these two numbers (§3.5):

Δ = (b − c) / n    e.g. Llama ASR: b=1, c=9, n=200 → Δ = (1−9)/200 = −0.040

💡 Why a matched-pair design for THIS project?
  1. The research question is causal-ish: “does quantization change behaviour?” Only the loading precision may differ, otherwise the comparison is uninterpretable. Sharing the checkpoint revision kills checkpoint-provenance confounds (a public pre-quantized GGUF file might differ in tokenizer, template, or revision).
  2. Safety outcomes are rare and noisy. With baseline ASRs from 0.07 to 0.585, unpaired comparisons would need far more GPU-hours to reach the same precision. Pairing buys statistical efficiency for free: the report’s §3.1 states pairing “increases statistical efficiency and gives discordant counts a direct interpretation.”
  3. It dictates the correct tests downstream. Because data are paired, the right tools are the paired bootstrap and McNemar’s test — using generic unpaired formulas would throw away the design and overstate noise.

2.3 The other controls hiding in the design

  • Revision pinning: configs record the exact Hugging Face commit hash, so “the same model” stays literally true even if upstream re-uploads.
  • Greedy decoding (temperature 0): the model always picks its top word, so rerunning gives byte-identical output — removes generation randomness from the primary study.
  • Fixed seed 42: any residual randomness (e.g. sampling in scorers/dataloaders) is reproducible.
  • Generation budget 512 tokens: fixed cap, because (as Chapter 10 tells) a shorter cap created a famous artefact.

Effect size

The delta: one number for “what changed”

Plain English
Every result row in Tables 4.1/4.2 starts with the simplest statistic imaginable: subtract the baseline rate from the quantized rate. That difference is called the effect size or delta (Δ) — “how much did quantization move this dial?”
ΔASR = ASR(NF4) − ASR(fp16) e.g. 0.060 − 0.100 = −0.040 (Llama)
ΔOR = OR(NF4) − OR(fp16) e.g. 0.080 − 0.128 = −0.048 (Phi-4-mini)
ΔMMLU = acc(NF4)− acc(fp16) e.g. 0.553 − 0.643 = −0.090 (Qwen3-1.7B)

3.1 Sign conventions — read these before reading any table

  • ΔASR positive ⇒ worse (more harmful compliance). ΔASR negative ⇒ safer-looking.
  • ΔOR (over-refusal) positive ⇒ worse (more benign prompts refused).
  • Capability Δ negative ⇒ worse (accuracy lost).

Your report says this explicitly (§4.1): positive safety deltas are undesirable increases in risk; negative capability deltas are undesirable losses. Keep it next to Table 4.1 while reading.

3.2 Same size delta ≠ same meaning

A ΔASR of +0.02 means different things at different baselines. Mistral sits at 0.585 — a +0.02 there is a 3% relative uptick on an already-bad rate. Phi sits at 0.070, so +0.02 would be a 29% relative increase. This is why your report reports paired absolute deltas per model and refuses to rank families on raw deltas (§4.5).

⚠️ Common trap #2 — a big delta is not automatically “real”
Δ = −0.040 could be a genuine shift, or ordinary sample wobble (Widget 1!). The delta answers “how big?”; Chapters 4–6 answer “is it distinguishable from wobble?”. You always need both halves: magnitude and uncertainty.
📌 Where this appears in your report
Every “Δ…” column of Tables 4.1 & 4.2; Figure 1.1 plots ΔMMLU against ΔASR per pair; the five interpretation labels (broad degradation etc., §3.5 end) are just rules combining the signs of several deltas.

Uncertainty, part I

The paired bootstrap: inventing parallel universes with a computer

You ran one experiment. You got one Δ = −0.090 for Qwen3-1.7B MMLU. But Widget 1 taught you a second batch of 300 questions would have given some other number. So the honest question is: which range of Δ values is consistent with the data we saw? That range is the confidence interval, and the bootstrap is how your project computes it.

The idea in one breath
Treat your own 300 paired results as a fake universe. Resample from it — draw 300 pairs with replacement, where the same pair can be picked twice or not at all — recompute Δ in that resampled dataset, and repeat thousands of times. The spread of those thousands of Δs shows how much Δ wobbles across universes. No formulas about populations needed: the data speak for themselves by being re-shuffled.

4.1 The recipe, line by line (this IS the method — nothing hidden)

✍️ Derivation / algorithm — exactly what compute_paired_bootstrap_ci() does
  1. Lay out the n paired outcomes side by side: (fp16ᵢ, NF4ᵢ) for i = 1…n. n = 300 for Qwen MMLU.
  2. Draw n indices uniformly with replacement. Some prompts picked 0×, some 1×, a few 2–3×. Critically, the SAME index selects BOTH members — this preserves the pairing.
  3. In that resample compute Δ* = mean(NF4) − mean(fp16).
  4. Repeat B times. Your repo uses B = 10,000 for the judge-scored HarmBench CIs (scripts/judge_agreement.py) and B = 2,000 as the helper default, with RNG seed 42 for reproducibility.
  5. Sort the B values of Δ*. Chop off the bottom 2.5% and top 2.5%. What remains is the 95% percentile confidence interval — literally “the middle 95% of parallel-universe deltas.”
Qwen3-1.7B MMLU: observed Δ = −0.090, bootstrap 95% CI = [−0.140, −0.043]
read: “universes compatible with our data mostly put the true loss between 4.3 and 14 points.”

4.2 Reading a CI like an adult

  • Zero outside the CI ⇒ the data are incompatible with “no change at the 5% level” — roughly matching p<0.05.
  • Zero inside the CI ⇒ “no change” remains plausible ⇒ non-significant. E.g. Phi ΔASR [−0.015, +0.055] straddles zero.
  • Narrow CI ⇒ precise estimate (ARC’s n=1172 gives tight CIs like [−0.0486, −0.0128]); wide CI ⇒ noisy (HarmBench’s n=200 gives wide ones like Mistral’s [−0.080, +0.040]).
  • A 95% CI does not mean “the true value has a 95% chance of sitting in this particular interval.” It means: the procedure, repeated across hypothetical studies, captures the truth 95% of the time. (Fine print, but examiners love asking.)
💡 Why the bootstrap for THIS project?
  1. Rerunning is impossible; simulating reruns is cheap. Each real replicate costs V100-hours on TC1. The bootstrap fabricates 10,000 statistically plausible replicates from the data you already own — uncertainty quantification without a single extra GPU-second.
  2. No distributional assumptions. ASR sits near 0 for some pairs (floor effects), CIs will be lopsided/skewed there, and textbook normal-approximation intervals get unreliable near boundaries. The percentile bootstrap doesn’t care about shapes.
  3. Pairing must survive resampling. A naive bootstrap that shuffles the two arms independently would destroy the matched design and wildly overstate uncertainty. Your repo’s implementation resamples pairs by index — the docstring in compare_quant_pairs.py spells this out. This is the “paired bootstrap” phrase in §3.5.

⚠️ Common trap #3 — “bootstrap = adding data”
No new information is created. Resampling re-cycles the same 300 outcomes, so the bootstrap measures spread; it cannot shrink it. Want narrower CIs? Collect more prompts (that’s power analysis, Chapter 8), not more resamples. (More resamples only make the CI edges numerically stabler.)

Uncertainty, part II

McNemar’s exact test: judging only the disagreements

The bootstrap gave a range for Δ. The McNemar test gives a p-value: a probability that directly answers “if NF4 truly changed nothing, how easily would luck alone produce flip counts this lopsided?”

The beautiful core idea
If the treatment does nothing, then for every prompt where the two arms disagree, the disagreement direction is a fair coin: 50/50 up vs down. So under the null hypothesis, b (ups) among the b+c disagreements behaves exactly like the number of heads in (b+c) fair coin tosses. Everything else follows from counting coin-toss outcomes — no approximations, hence “exact” test.

5.1 Derivation you can do on paper

✍️ From coin flips to a p-value in five steps
  1. Throw away all concordant pairs (both-yes, both-no). Keep the b + c discordant ones. Llama ASR: b=1, c=9 → 10 disagreements.
  2. H₀ (“NF4 changes nothing”) says each disagreement is equally likely to be an up or a down: P(up)=0.5.
  3. The probability of seeing exactly k ups out of N=b+c fair coins is C(N,k) · 0.5ᴺ, where C(N,k) = “N choose k” counts the arrangements.
  4. “At least as lopsided as observed” = k ≤ min(b,c) or k ≥ max(b,c). By symmetry, double the lower tail:
  5. p = min(1, 2 × Σₖ₌₀..min(b,c) C(N,k)·0.5ᴺ) — precisely what mcnemar_exact_test() computes in your repo (no SciPy needed).
Worked example — Llama ASR, N=10, min(b,c)=1:
P(≤1 head in 10 tosses) = [C(10,0)+C(10,1)] / 2¹⁰ = (1+10)/1024 = 0.01074.
Two-sided p = 2 × 0.01074 = 0.0215. ✔ matches Table 4.1 exactly.

💡 Why McNemar’s exact test for THIS project?
  1. The data are paired binary outcomes. The textbook two-proportion z-test assumes independent samples; using it here would treat shared prompt difficulty as noise, inflating variance and (worse) breaking the logic. McNemar is THE canonical paired-binary test.
  2. Exact beats approximate when disagreements are scarce. Safety outcomes are rare: Phi had only 14 discordant prompts, XSTest contrasts often <10. Normal approximations are shaky at those counts; the exact binomial needs none.
  3. It matches the design’s own currency. §3.1 promised “discordant counts get a direct interpretation.” McNemar delivers literally that: its entire statistic IS the discordant split (b vs c), so reviewers can audit it by eye.
  4. Two instruments, one conclusion. CI and p answer related-but-different questions (how big? vs how surprising?). Your report deliberately reports both (§3.5: “a confidence interval and an exact paired test answer related but not identical questions”), e.g. Qwen-4B ΔASR has CI touching zero AND p=0.0963 — consistent stories, different lenses.

5.2 A 90-second honesty course on p-values

  • A p-value is P(data this extreme | H₀ true) — it is not P(H₀ true | data). Nobody can compute that latter quantity without extra assumptions about prior likelihoods.
  • p = 0.03 does not mean “97% chance the effect is real.” It means: if nothing were going on, data at least this dramatic happen 3% of the time.
  • Failing to reach p<0.05 is not evidence of zero effect (Widget 1 wobble; Chapter 8 power).
  • p-hackable: test 20 things, some will dip below 0.05 by pure luck — which is exactly why Chapter 6 exists.
⚠️ Common trap #4 — “p = 1.000 means proven identical”
Qwen-1.7B’s McNemar p = 1.0000 means the observed split (16/16) is maximally boring under the null. It does NOT prove the models are identical — with more prompts you’d surely find small imbalances. Your report handles this properly by never claiming “proven preservation”; it says the data provide no evidence of increase, and Chapter 9 uses TOST when it wants to argue positive closeness.

Decision, part I

Testing 20 things at once: the look-elsewhere effect & Benjamini–Hochberg

Your primary family = 5 pairs × 4 outcomes = 20 hypothesis tests. Each test uses the “p<0.05 ⇒ interesting” rule, which is wrong 1-in-20 of the time even when nothing real is happening. Twenty chances at a 5% fluke is not a 5% fluke anymore.

Plain English
If you pull one slot-machine lever, a jackpot probably means something. Pull 20 levers and one rings anyway. A scientist who only reports the ringing lever has discovered nothing. Multiple-comparison correction is the accounting that separates “rang because rigged” from “rang because you pulled 20 times.”

Theory: E[false discoveries] = 20 × 0.05 = 1 per experiment; P(at least one) = 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%.

6.1 Two philosophies of correction

ApproachControlsGuaranteeCost
Bonferroni (family-wise)chance of ANY false discoveryfalse-alarm rate ≤ α for whole familybrutal: threshold becomes 0.05/20 = 0.0025 → real effects get missed
Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR)proportion of discoveries that are falseamong your claimed discoveries, expected fake share ≤ q (5%)gentler: keeps power to detect real effects while capping fake-news fraction

Your report chose FDR control [ref 49]. In an exploratory benchmark matrix where several genuine effects are plausible (capability losses), FDR’s bargain — “allow maybe one of our three survivors to be noise, on average” — fits better than Bonferroni’s near-guaranteed blindness.

6.2 The BH recipe, step by step

✍️ The step-up procedure (this exact algorithm lives in benjamini_hochberg())
  1. Collect all m=20 p-values. Sort ascending: p₍₁₎ ≤ p₍₂₎ ≤ … ≤ p₍₂₀₎.
  2. Give each rank k a threshold: (k/m) × q, q = 0.05. Rank 1 must beat 0.0025, rank 10 must beat 0.025, rank 20 must beat 0.05.
  3. Walk ranks from largest to smallest and find the BIGGEST k whose p₍ₖ₎ ≤ its threshold.
  4. Declare significant every test with rank ≤ that k. Everything else fails.
  5. (For reporting, each p also gets an adjusted q-value: qᵢ = min over j≥i of [ p₍ⱼ₎·m/j ], forced non-decreasing — the number you compare directly against 0.05.)

💡 Why BH-FDR for THIS project?
  1. The design multiplies tests by construction. Five models × four outcomes was chosen for scientific coverage, not to farm p-values — but reviewers correctly demand the bill be paid. Declaring the family of 20 up front (§3.5: “the primary NF4 set of corrected comparisons contains 20 contrasts”) makes the correction principled rather than post-hoc.
  2. Safety conclusions must be extra conservative, but not blind. The scary claim would be “quantization increases harm.” With no true effect anywhere on the ASR axis, raw testing still coughed up one significant-looking decrease (Llama). FDR control is what lets the report say “no ASR contrast survives” honestly while still detecting the two large capability losses.
  3. FDR matches how readers consume a table. Readers scan Table 4.x for bold/significant rows. Controlling the expected fake-share among those rows (≤5%) is exactly the property a scanning reader needs. Bonferroni would instead make even the −0.090 MMLU drop borderline (its q=0.008 passes Bonferroni too here, but marginal ones like Phi OR wouldn’t).
  4. Uncorrected results stay visible but powerless. The report shows raw p alongside q so readers see the full evidence gradient (§3.5: “uncorrected significance remains visible… but does not determine the study’s main conclusion”).
⚠️ Common trap #5 — “q = 0.24 means we’re 76% sure of nothing” / mixing p and q
A q-value is the p-value’s post-correction price tag: “if you called everything at this q or smaller, about 24% of those calls would be false discoveries on average.” It never means “24% chance this specific finding is fake.” And never mix systems: compare p to α for a single pre-declared test; compare q to q-level when the family was tested jointly — which is always the case in this report.

Measurement validity

Cohen’s kappa: do two raters agree beyond luck?

Your report’s statistical correction machinery is useless if the ruler itself is broken. So the project validates scorers (HarmBench classifier, refusal regex, gpt-4o judge, LlamaGuard, humans) against each other. The headline coefficient is Cohen’s kappa (κ) [ref 50] — agreement with chance-guessing subtracted out.

Why raw % agreement lies
Two scorers that both flag almost nothing will “agree” hugely just by both saying no all day. Mistral’s over-refusal sits at 0.004 — two lazy scorers would hit ~99% agreement there while agreeing about nothing. Kappa fixes this by asking: how much better-than-coin-flip is the agreement?

7.1 Derivation on a tiny example

✍️ κ from scratch with 10 prompts

Suppose a regex and a human classify 10 responses as Refuse/Comply:

observed agreement  Po = fraction of items where they match

chance agreement    Pe = Σ over categories of (row-total/N × col-total/N)
    (the probability two coin-flippers using each rater’s own base rates collide on the same guess)

κ = (Po − Pe) / (1 − Pe)   → “agreement above luck, as a share of maximum possible above-luck”

Mini-case: human refuses 5/10, regex flags 4/10; they agree-refuse on 3 and agree-comply on 4 → Po=7/10=0.70. Pe = (5×4)/100 + (5×6)/100 = 0.20+0.30 = 0.50. κ = (0.70−0.50)/(1−0.50) = 0.40. Raw said 70%; chance-corrected says “moderate.” Both numbers matter — your report always shows them together.

κLandis–Koch label

Your report cites these bands but explicitly refuses to apply them mechanically [refs 53, 54]: κ depends on prevalence and on how many categories exist, so context beats labels.

And negative κ (regex −0.006!) doesn’t mean “anti-correlated”; near zero means “no better than guessing from marginals.” Your report therefore pairs every κ with counts and task context instead of adjectives.

Decision quality

Power & MDE: what could this study even see?

Chapter 1 warned: non-significant ≠ absent. Power analysis turns that warning into arithmetic. First, the error grid:

Reality: no changeReality: real change
You say “significant”Type I error (false alarm) — probability α, policed by p-values & BH✅ correct detection — probability power
You say “not significant”✅ correct null callType II error (missed it) — probability β; power = 1−β
Plain English
Power is the smoke detector’s sensitivity: with a good detector (power 0.8) a real fire gets an alarm 80% of the time; with a weak one (power 0.2) fires burn quietly and you wrongly conclude “no fire here” most of the time. Power grows with sample size n, with bigger true effects, and with less noise.

8.1 The Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)

✍️ Your repo’s formula (mde_mcnemar(), Connor 1987 / Lachin normal approx.)
MDE ≈ (z1−α/2 + z1−β) × √(ψ / n)

ψ (psi) = discordant rate = (b+c)/n  — how often the pair members disagree at all
z1−α/2 = 1.960 (for α=0.05 two-sided)  ·  z1−β = 0.842 (for power 0.80)

intuition: √(ψ/n) is the standard error of Δ ⇒ MDE ≈ 2.8 × SE ⇒
“effects smaller than ~2.8 standard errors usually slip past a 5% test only half the time or less”

HarmBench reality check (from the artifact): median discordant rate ψ=0.09 at n=200 → MDE ≈ 0.059. So at this benchmark size, only ASR shifts of roughly ±6 points or larger were reliably detectable. Every observed NF4 ASR delta sat below that floor (largest: +0.040) — which reframes the null: partly genuine smallness, partly detector limits. That is precisely §4.4’s sentence “smaller observed deltas… unresolved rather than claims of equivalence.”

Drag n and discordant rate; the gauge shows the smallest Δ an 80%-powered study could catch. Presets use the artifact’s per-pair discordant rates.
💡 Why power/MDE analysis for THIS project?
  1. The headline is a NULL. “No significant harm increase” invites the sneaky misreading “so quantization is proven safe.” Quantifying the detection floor (±~0.06 ASR) states honestly what “no evidence of increase” can and cannot exclude — the report calls this “absence of significance bounded by power, not proof of a zero effect.”
  2. It arbitrates between benchmarks. Qwen-4B ARC −0.0162 reached p=0.042 (n=1172, tight CI) while similar-sized safety deltas stayed ambiguous (n=200, wide CIs): §4.4 attributes the difference to sample size and multiplicity, not model behaviour.
  3. Discipline against post-hoc storytelling. Card et al. [52] warn that computing power AFTER seeing significance is fallacious; the project computes prospective-style MDE floors from design quantities (ψ, n) and lets them annotate — not replace — the tests.
  4. It guides future work. Want to resolve a suspected +0.03 ASR shift? Invert the formula: you’d need roughly n ≈ ψ·((2.8)/0.03)² ≈ 280 paired prompts at ψ=0.09 — i.e. a bigger HarmBench sample or replicated seeds.

Decision, part II

TOST: proving things are close, not merely “not different”

§4.6 wants to claim INT8 keeps capability essentially intact. A p>0.05 can’t carry that claim (trap #4). The formal tool is equivalence testing via the Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) procedure [ref 56].

Plain English
Instead of asking “is the difference zero?” (impossible to prove), ask two flipped questions: (a) “can we reject that the effect is worse than −m points?”, and (b) “can we reject that it is better than +m points?” If BOTH nulls die at α, the effect is confidently trapped inside (−m, +m) — practically equivalent within margin m. Your margin: m = ±0.05, pre-declared as “a five-point capability change is the smallest that would alter a deployment decision between two precisions of the same checkpoint” (capability_equivalence.py docstring).
✍️ Mechanics (and the shortcut everyone actually uses)
H₁: d ≤ −m  test: p_lower = Φ((d+m)/se)    reject if p_lower < α
H₂: d ≥ +m  test: p_upper = Φ((d−m)/se)    reject if p_upper < α
p_TOST = max(p_lower, p_upper) < 0.05 ⇔ equivalence declared

shortcut: equivalence holds ⇔ the 90% CI for d lies entirely inside (−m,+m)
(why 90%? each one-sided test uses α=0.05 ⇒ combined confidence 1−2α)

The repo computes paired SE directly from the discordant cells: Var = [(b+c) − (b−c)²/n]/n². Verdict for the INT8 capability contrasts: equivalent within ±0.05 — a positive, defensible closeness claim, unlike any number of p=0.43 hand-waves.

9.1 Three-way verdict map — the mental picture worth memorising

💡 Why TOST for THIS project?
  1. A deployment recommendation needs a closeness guarantee. “INT8 didn’t significantly hurt anything” is weak tea; engineers choosing a precision want “stays within half a point… five points of fp16.” TOST delivers the second sentence.
  2. Asymmetry with the safety axis is informative. Capability: equivalence supported at INT8. Safety: no equivalence claim attempted (equivalence of harm rates isn’t the right question there — the study asks about increases). Declaring margins only where meaningful is part of §3.5’s “equivalence assessed only where the pre-specified margin and test are appropriate.”
  3. Pre-declared margins beat post-hoc vibes. Fixing ±0.05 BEFORE seeing results (and labelling the alternative “tightest common margin” as descriptive-only, per the module docstring) is exactly the preregistration hygiene the rest of the report models.
📌 Where this appears in your report

Implementation: tost() in scripts/capability_equivalence.py, with Z₉₀ = 1.6449 and the degenerate zero-variance guard for contrast pairs with zero discordance.

Robustness

The sensitivity toolkit: attacking your own conclusion

RQ5 asks whether conclusions survive changes of scorer, budget and seed. Each check is itself a small statistical idea:

10.1 Seeds — measuring generation randomness instead of ignoring it

Primary runs are greedy (deterministic), so a single run has no sampling noise — but a deployed model won’t be greedy. So the project re-runs five pairs with temperature sampling across seeds 1–5 (sensitivity_multiseed.json) and reports whether greedy deltas sit inside the seed spread. It’s a poor-man’s variance estimate: treat seeds as replicates, check the greedy point estimate against their min–max range. §6.6.1/§4.x honestly notes two cross-family greedy deltas sit just outside their seed ranges — robustness evidence with its warts shown.

10.2 Generation budget — the truncation-artifact detective story

An earlier 128-token era showed a scary Qwen-1.7B ΔASR of +0.055. Forensics: measure how often 128-token responses were cut mid-answer (60.3% carried truncation markers, established by a direct-prefix test comparing 128-token outputs to their 512-token continuations in genlen_robustness.json), then re-run everything at HarmBench’s reference 512-token budget — the anomaly evaporates. Statistical moral: a measurement artefact can manufacture significance; budgets are part of the measurement instrument. This is why the primary study is pinned at 512 and the 128 tree is kept only as a scoped comparison (§4.6).

10.4 Floor & ceiling effects — why Mistral’s over-refusal row is boring on purpose

Mistral’s OR is 0.004 in both arms (1 prompt of 250!). With almost nothing to detect, deltas are pinned near zero regardless of truth, discordant counts shrink (McNemar loses material), and κ values get depressed by skew (Ch. 7). Recognising floor effects stops you from narrating “no change” where “cannot see” is the honest statement.

💡 Why so much robustness machinery?
Because every earlier chapter protects against random error (wobble), but robustness checks protect against systematic error: a quirky scorer, a stingy token budget, one lucky seed, floor-pinned outcomes. Statistics’ formal tests assume the measurement is sound; in an LLM-behaviour study — where the “instrument” is another language model or a regex — that assumption must be bought with evidence. This is the report’s deepest habit: no headline without a stress test.
