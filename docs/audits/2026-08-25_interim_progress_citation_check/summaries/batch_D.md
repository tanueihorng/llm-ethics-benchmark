## [13] TrustLLM: Trustworthiness in Large Language Models
- **Bibliography entry:** [13] Y. Huang, L. Sun, et al., “Position: TrustLLM: Trustworthiness in large language models,” in Proc. International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), PMLR vol. 235, 2024. arXiv:2401.05561.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[20] Huang et al. - Position - TrustLLM - Trustworthiness in large language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed. The title page states "Proceedings of the 41st International Conference on Machine Learning, Vienna, Austria. PMLR 235, 2024", matching the bibliography's ICML venue.
- **Problem & context:** Trustworthy LLMs lack an accepted, unified definition and a comprehensive evaluation suite; prior taxonomies either miss aspects of trustworthiness or split it into overlapping subcategories that are hard to benchmark.
- **Approach:** The authors review about 500 recent papers and propose principles spanning eight dimensions of trustworthiness: truthfulness, safety, fairness, robustness, privacy, machine ethics, transparency, and accountability. Because transparency and accountability resist benchmarking, they build a benchmark over the other six dimensions, comprising more than 18 subcategories and over 30 datasets mixing classification and generation tasks, several contributed as new datasets such as JailbreakTrigger. They evaluate 16 mainstream LLMs, both proprietary and open-weight. Within safety, jailbreak resilience is tested with 13 attack methods organized into a taxonomy of five classes and 13 subclasses, exaggerated safety is measured on XSTest-style prompts, and misuse uses Do-Not-Answer and related sets.
- **Key findings:** Trustworthiness and capability are positively correlated, and trustworthiness rankings often mirror capability leaderboards (§2.1). Proprietary models generally beat open-weight ones, yet the Llama2 series surpasses proprietary models on many tasks without external moderator modules (§2.1). Many models are overly aligned: Llama2-7b refuses 57% of prompts that are in fact not harmful (§2.1), and Table 17 shows the Llama2 series has the most severe exaggerated-safety problem (Appendix E.2).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.1 Trustworthiness and Safety Evaluation

## [14] DecodingTrust: A Comprehensive Assessment of Trustworthiness in GPT Models
- **Bibliography entry:** [14] B. Wang et al., “DecodingTrust: A comprehensive assessment of trustworthiness in GPT models,” in Proc. NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks Track, 2023. arXiv:2306.11698.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[21] Wang et al. - DecodingTrust - A comprehensive assessment of trustworthiness in GPT models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed. The first page reads "37th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2023) Track on Datasets and Benchmarks", matching the cited venue.
- **Problem & context:** GPT models were being proposed for sensitive applications while the literature on their trustworthiness remained limited and fragmented across single-perspective evaluations such as robustness alone.
- **Approach:** DecodingTrust is a unified evaluation platform combining existing and newly generated challenging datasets across eight perspectives: toxicity, stereotype bias, adversarial robustness, out-of-distribution robustness, robustness to adversarial demonstrations, privacy, machine ethics, and fairness. The core comparison is GPT-4 versus GPT-3.5 under varied system and user prompts, with extensions to open models including Llama, Llama 2, Alpaca, and Red Pajama in Appendix L. Adversarial cases include jailbreaking system prompts, misleading targeted system prompts, attacks transferred from an Alpaca-7B attacker, backdoored demonstrations, and PII extraction with email-domain hints.
- **Key findings:** Under benign prompts GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 keep toxicity probability below 32%, but carefully designed adversarial jailbreaking prompts push it to almost 100% (Introduction findings list). GPT-4 is usually more trustworthy than GPT-3.5 on standard benchmarks, yet is more vulnerable given jailbreaking prompts, plausibly because it follows misleading instructions more precisely (Abstract). Among transferred attacks, SemAttack reaches 89.2% attack success rate on GPT-4 (Introduction). For privacy, email extraction accuracy can rise 100-fold with supplementary domain knowledge under few-shot prompting (Introduction).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.1 Trustworthiness and Safety Evaluation

## [15] SafetyBench: Evaluating the Safety of Large Language Models
- **Bibliography entry:** [15] Z. Zhang et al., “SafetyBench: Evaluating the safety of large language models,” in Proc. Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL), 2024. arXiv:2309.07045.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[22] Zhang et al. - SafetyBench - Evaluating the safety of large language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed. The running head shows "Proceedings of the 62nd Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (Volume 1: Long Papers), pages 15537–15553, August 11-16, 2024", matching the cited ACL venue.
- **Problem & context:** Comprehensive safety benchmarks were scarce: existing datasets covered single concerns such as toxicity or bias, manual evaluation was too slow for iteration, and few safety classifiers generalized across many harm categories.
- **Approach:** SafetyBench is, per the authors, the first comprehensive multiple-choice benchmark for LLM safety. It contains 11,435 questions spanning seven categories of safety issues, derived from eight typical scenarios with the politically divergent Sensitive Topics category dropped, sourced from existing Chinese and English datasets, safety-related exams, and ChatGPT-augmented samples, all human verified. Questions are bilingual Chinese and English. The authors evaluate 25 popular LLMs in zero-shot and few-shot settings and additionally compare multiple-choice accuracy against safe-response ratios under constrained and open-ended generation for six models.
- **Key findings:** GPT-4 stands out with a substantial margin over all counterparts, and open-sourced LLMs show a significant gap to it, indicating ample room for improvement (Figure 2, Conclusion). Multiple-choice scores correlate with generation behavior: the system-level Pearson correlation between accuracy and constrained-query safe-response ratios is 0.99, and 0.91 for open-ended queries (Table 5 discussion, §5.1). Safe-response ratios on open-ended queries sit well above multiple-choice accuracy, so the question format exposes hidden safety risks that open-ended probing misses (§5.1).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.1 Trustworthiness and Safety Evaluation

## [17] Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models
- **Bibliography entry:** [17] A. Zou, Z. Wang, N. Carlini, M. Nasr, J. Z. Kolter, and M. Fredrikson, “Universal and transferable adversarial attacks on aligned language models,” 2023. arXiv:2307.15043 (preprint).
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[25] Zou et al. - Universal and transferable adversarial attacks on aligned language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** arXiv preprint (no archival venue shown). The extract carries only the stamp "arXiv:2307.15043v2 [cs.CL] 20 Dec 2023"; all venue strings found belong to reference-list entries, not this paper.
- **Problem & context:** Known jailbreaks relied on manual ingenuity and were brittle, while prior automatic prompt searches had proven unreliable against aligned LLMs because discrete token inputs make optimization hard.
- **Approach:** The paper introduces an automated suffix attack that appends optimized tokens to a user query so the model starts with an affirmative response such as "Sure, here is", switching it into complying mode. Optimization combines greedy and gradient-guided discrete search: gradients nominate promising single-token replacements at every position, candidate substitutions are evaluated, and the best is kept. Reliability comes from training one universal suffix jointly across many harmful prompts and multiple open models, specifically Vicuna-13B, Vicuna-7B, and Guanaco-7B. The resulting suffixes are then tested black-box on commercial systems.
- **Key findings:** Against Vicuna, the attack elicits 99 of 100 harmful behaviors and produces 88 of 100 exact matches to target output strings (§1). Transferred suffixes achieve up to 84% success against GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 and 66% against PaLM-2, while Claude stays much lower at 2.1% though still yields otherwise unseen behavior (§1). Prior optimizers PEZ and GBDA produce no exact matches and AutoPrompt reaches only 25%, versus 88% for this method (§1). Results were disclosed to OpenAI, Google, Meta, and Anthropic before release.
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.1 Trustworthiness and Safety Evaluation

## [18] Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models
- **Bibliography entry:** [18] E. Perez, S. Huang, F. Song, T. Cai, R. Ring, J. Aslanides, A. Glaese, N. McAleese, and G. Irving, “Red teaming language models with language models,” in Proc. Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing (EMNLP), pp. 3419–3448, 2022, doi: 10.18653/v1/2022.emnlp-main.225.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[26] Perez et al. - Red teaming language models with language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed. The footer shows "Proceedings of the 2022 Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing, pages 3419–3448, December 7-11, 2022", matching the cited EMNLP entry and pages.
- **Problem & context:** Pre-deployment safety testing depended on human annotators handwriting test cases, which limited how many and how diverse the discovered failures could be, as the Tay incident illustrated.
- **Approach:** The authors generate natural-language test cases with another language model, query a target LM, and flag failures with classifiers that detect harmful outputs, requiring no white-box access or differentiability. The main target is the 280B-parameter Dialogue-Prompted Gopher chatbot. Test-case generation spans zero-shot sampling, few-shot sampling, supervised learning on human-written Bot Adversarial Dialog questions, and reinforcement learning with KL penalties. Beyond offensive-content detection, prompted generation targets training-data leakage, fabricated contact information, distributional bias against named groups, and full multi-turn dialogues.
- **Key findings:** Half a million zero-shot test cases elicit offensive replies 3.7% of the time, yielding 18,444 failed cases, while reinforcement learning with low KL penalty (alpha 0.3) raises the offensive-reply rate above 40% (§3.2, Figure 2). Generated cases compare favorably with the hand-written BAD set in diversity and difficulty. The study further uncovers replies leaking memorized training data, chatbots giving real phone numbers including suicide-hotline and hospital numbers as their own contact details, and dialogues where offensive replies beget further offensive replies (§4, §5, Appendix B).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.1 Trustworthiness and Safety Evaluation

## [38] A StrongREJECT for Empty Jailbreaks
- **Bibliography entry:** [38] A. Souly et al., “A StrongREJECT for empty jailbreaks,” in Proc. NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks Track, 2024, doi: 10.52202/079017-3984.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[41] Souly et al. - A StrongREJECT for empty jailbreaks.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed. The first page reads "38th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2024) Track on Datasets and Benchmarks", matching the cited venue and DOI source.
- **Problem & context:** Jailbreak papers routinely claim near-100% attack success, but many claimed jailbreaks elicit vacuous or useless answers. The root cause is the absence of a standard high-quality benchmark: existing prompt sets are repetitive or answerable without harm, and automated evaluators score mere non-refusal rather than useful compliance.
- **Approach:** StrongREJECT pairs a dataset of forbidden prompts that demand specific, verifiable harmful information across six universally prohibited content categories with an automated evaluator scoring how much useful information a response provides, capturing both willingness and capability; rubric-based and fine-tuned variants are released. Agreement with human labels is benchmarked against prior evaluators. Effectiveness is then re-measured for 37 jailbreak methods on 313 forbidden prompts against GPT-4o, GPT-3.5 Turbo, and Llama-3.1 70B Instruct, with capability experiments on the unaligned Dolphin model and on GPT-4o answering MMLU items rewritten by each jailbreak.
- **Key findings:** Existing evaluators systematically overstate jailbreak effectiveness relative to human judgment, especially string-matched non-refusal (Table 1, §3). Both StrongREJECT evaluators attain state-of-the-art agreement, lowest mean absolute error and the highest Spearman correlation with human jailbreak rankings, with HarmBench comparable (Table 1, Figure 2). The most effective attacks are LLM-driven ones, PAIR and PAP (§4, Figure 3). Jailbreaks that raise willingness tend to lower capability, shown on Dolphin and on GPT-4o MMLU scores (Figure 4).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.4 Refusal, Harmfulness, and Threat Models

## [39] SORRY-Bench: Systematically Evaluating Large Language Model Safety Refusal
- **Bibliography entry:** [39] T. Xie et al., “SORRY-Bench: Systematically evaluating large language model safety refusal,” in Proc. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR), 2025.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[42] Xie et al. - SORRY-Bench - Systematically evaluating large language model safety refusal.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed. The running head on page 1 reads "Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2025", matching the cited ICLR venue.
- **Problem & context:** Existing safety-refusal evaluations use coarse and inconsistent taxonomies that over-represent some topics, ignore linguistic variety in how users phrase requests, and lean on expensive GPT-scale judges.
- **Approach:** The authors unify ten prior taxonomies through a human-in-the-loop procedure into a 44-class taxonomy across four high-level domains, then craft a class-balanced base set of 440 unsafe instructions, ten per category. Twenty linguistic mutations, covering styles, persuasion techniques, encodings such as ASCII and Caesar, and languages including Tamil and French, expand it by 8.8K instructions. Using over 7K collected human annotations they meta-evaluate LLM-as-judge designs and distill a fast evaluator, then benchmark over 50 proprietary and open-weight LLMs on refusal behavior.
- **Key findings:** Fine-tuned 7B judges reach above 80% agreement with humans, comparable to or surpassing GPT-4o scale models, at roughly tens of seconds per evaluation pass on a single A100 (Abstract, §1). Across models, Claude-2 and Gemini-1.5 exhibit the most refusals while Mistral models comply with potentially unsafe requests at markedly higher rates (§1). Fulfillment varies substantially across the 20 mutations, showing current models are inconsistent for low-resource languages, technical terms, and uncommon dialects (§1, §4).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.4 Refusal, Harmfulness, and Threat Models

## [40] JailbreakBench: An Open Robustness Benchmark for Jailbreaking Large Language Models
- **Bibliography entry:** [40] P. Chao et al., “JailbreakBench: An open robustness benchmark for jailbreaking large language models,” in Proc. NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks Track, 2024.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[43] Chao et al. - JailbreakBench - An open robustness benchmark for jailbreaking large language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed. The first page reads "38th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2024) Track on Datasets and Benchmarks", matching the cited venue.
- **Problem & context:** Jailbreak evaluation lacked standards of practice: costs and success rates were computed inconsistently, and many studies were irreproducible because they withheld adversarial prompts, closed their code, or leaned on shifting proprietary APIs.
- **Approach:** JailbreakBench contributes four parts: an evolving repository of jailbreak artifacts storing prompts, responses, and metadata; the JBB-Behaviors dataset of 100 unique harmful behaviors in ten categories aligned with OpenAI usage policies, of which 18% come from AdvBench, 27% from TDC/HarmBench, and 55% are original, paired with 100 topic-matched benign behaviors for refusal checks; a standardized red-teaming and defense-evaluation pipeline with fixed threat model, chat templates, and decoding parameters, shipping five baseline defenses; and a public leaderboard. A human evaluation of six jailbreak classifiers selects Llama-3-Instruct-70B with a chosen prompt as the judge.
- **Key findings:** Under the standardized judge, GCG transfers poorly, reaching only 3% attack success on Llama-2 and 4% on GPT-4, whereas a prompt template with random search is strongest at 90% on Llama-2 and 78% on GPT-4; the popular AIM template fails entirely on Llama-2 and the GPT models (Table 2, §4). Among defenses, perplexity filtering helps only against GCG, SmoothLLM cuts GCG and PAIR, and Erase-and-Check is most solid while Prompt with RS keeps non-trivial success (Table 3). Undefended refusal rates differ sharply: 9% for Vicuna versus above 60% for Llama-2 (Figure 2).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.4 Refusal, Harmfulness, and Threat Models, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.6 Synthesis and Research Gap

## [41] WildGuard: Open One-stop Moderation Tools for Safety Risks, Jailbreaks, and Refusals of LLMs
- **Bibliography entry:** [41] S. Han et al., “WildGuard: Open one-stop moderation tools for safety risks, jailbreaks, and refusals of LLMs,” in Proc. NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks Track, 2024.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[44] Han et al. - WildGuard - Open one-stop moderation tools for safety risks, jailbreaks, and refusals of LLMs.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed. The first page reads "38th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2024) Track on Datasets and Benchmarks", matching the cited venue.
- **Problem & context:** Open moderation tools such as Llama-Guard2 lag far behind a prompted GPT-4 on adversarial jailbreaks, and none adequately performs the third task the authors isolate, determining whether a model refused, which matters for measuring over-refusal and safety behavior beyond raw harm classification.
- **Approach:** WildGuard is a lightweight moderator covering 13 risk categories that performs three tasks: detecting malicious intent in user prompts, detecting safety risks in responses, and determining refusal. Training uses WildGuardMix, a 92K-example multi-task dataset balancing vanilla and adversarial prompts paired with compliant and refusing responses, split into WildGuardTrain (87K) and a 5,299-item human-annotated WildGuardTest. The model is instruction-tuned from Mistral-7B-v0.3, with ablations showing the data, not the base choice, drives performance. Evaluation covers WildGuardTest and ten public benchmarks against ten open moderation baselines and a GPT-4 judge.
- **Key findings:** WildGuard sets state-of-the-art open-source results across all three tasks, improving refusal detection by up to 26.4 points over the strongest open baselines, and matches or exceeds GPT-4, by up to 3.9 points on adversarial prompt harmfulness identification (Abstract, §1). Deployed as a filter in an LLM interface it cuts jailbreak attack success from 79.8% to 2.4% without raising refusals of benign requests (Abstract, §4.4).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.4 Refusal, Harmfulness, and Threat Models, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.5 Automatic Evaluators and Human Validation

## [46] Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?
- **Bibliography entry:** [46] A. Wei, N. Haghtalab, and J. Steinhardt, “Jailbroken: How does LLM safety training fail?,” in Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS), vol. 36, 2023.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[48] Wei et al. - Jailbroken - How does LLM safety training fail.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed. The first page reads "37th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2023)", consistent with the cited volume 36.
- **Problem & context:** Safety-trained LLMs still fall to jailbreak attacks, yet the phenomenon lacked conceptual explanation; the paper asks why safety training fails and how failures can be anticipated rather than catalogued ad hoc.
- **Approach:** The authors hypothesize two failure modes: competing objectives, where capability goals conflict with safety goals, and mismatched generalization, where safety training does not cover domains the model's capabilities do. Guided by these they construct and adapt attacks including prefix injection, refusal suppression, obfuscations such as Base64, ROT13, leetspeak and disemvoweling, style injection, website-content generation, three escalating combination attacks, and model-assisted auto-obfuscation, alongside jailbreakchat.com prompts. They evaluate GPT-4, Claude v1.3, and GPT-3.5 Turbo on a curated set of 32 prompts drawn from OpenAI and Anthropic red-teaming material and a held-out synthetic set of 317 prompts, manually labeling outcomes as GoodBot, BadBot, or Unclear over 2,970 curated and 2,536 synthetic samples (§4.2).
- **Key findings:** New failure-mode attacks succeed on over 96% of evaluated prompts, including 100% of the curated red-teaming prompts the safety interventions were built against (Abstract, §1). On the held-out set the top combinations reach BadBot rates of 0.93 on GPT-4 and 0.89 on Claude v1.3, and an adaptive attack choosing among all tested jailbreaks reaches 0.96 and 0.99 respectively (Table 2, §4.3). Roleplay-style attacks score 0% on Claude, hinting targeted training helps locally while other strategies still succeed, supporting the argument for safety-capability parity rather than relying on scale (§4.3, §1).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.6 Synthesis and Research Gap
