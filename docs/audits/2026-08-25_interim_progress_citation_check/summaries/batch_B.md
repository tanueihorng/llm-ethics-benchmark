## [5] The Llama 3 herd of models
- **Bibliography entry:** [5] A. Grattafiori et al. (Meta AI), “The Llama 3 herd of models,” Meta AI, 2024. arXiv:2407.21783.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[02] Grattafiori et al. - The Llama 3 herd of models.pdf` (extracted text is arXiv:2407.21783v3, dated 23 Nov 2024; no alternate version file in the repo)
- **Publication status:** arXiv preprint (no archival venue shown)
- **Problem & context:** Modern AI systems rest on foundation models, but open models trail closed frontier systems in capability and safety tooling. Meta presents Llama 3, a herd of language models intended to narrow that gap while natively supporting multilinguality, coding, reasoning, and tool usage.
- **Approach:** The flagship is a standard dense Transformer with 405B parameters, pre-trained on about 15T multilingual tokens (15.6T text tokens) at roughly 3.8×10^25 FLOPs, then run through a continued pre-training stage that extends the context window to 128K tokens. Smaller 8B and 70B variants are trained well beyond compute-optimal budgets so they perform better at equal inference cost. Post-training proceeds in multiple rounds of supervised finetuning followed by Direct Preference Optimization, supported by a reward model, rejection sampling, and curated human preference data. Safety mitigations are folded into post-training, and the released package includes Llama Guard 3 for input and output safety. Image, video, and speech extensions via compositional encoders are reported but not released.
- **Key findings:** The abstract reports quality comparable to GPT-4 across many tasks, with Table 2 comparing the finetuned 8B, 70B, and 405B models against competitors on benchmarks including MMLU, GSM8K, ARC Challenge, and GPQA. In safety evaluations (§5.4) the team measures violation rate against false refusal rate on internal benchmarks totalling over 4000 prompts, reporting a competitive balance against anonymised API-based and open competitors (§5.4.4, Figure 21), while footnote 13 concedes those internal numbers are not externally reproducible.

## [6] Phi-4-Mini technical report
- **Bibliography entry:** [6] A. Abouelenin et al., “Phi-4-Mini technical report: Compact yet powerful multimodal language models via Mixture-of-LoRAs,” Microsoft, 2025. arXiv:2503.01743.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[03] Abouelenin et al. - Phi-4-Mini technical report - Compact yet powerful multimodal language models via Mixture-of-LoRAs.pdf` (extracted text is arXiv:2503.01743v2, dated 7 Mar 2025; no alternate version file in the repo)
- **Publication status:** arXiv preprint (no archival venue shown)
- **Problem & context:** Small language models must run cheaply yet usually trail larger ones on reasoning-heavy work. Microsoft extends the Phi family recipe of carefully curated synthetic data to a compact model and adds vision and speech without degrading its language ability.
- **Approach:** Phi-4-Mini is a decoder-only Transformer with 32 layers, hidden size 3072, a 200,064-entry vocabulary, grouped query attention (24 query heads, 8 key/value heads), and 128K context via LongRoPE (§2.1). Pre-training draws on a filtered five-trillion-token corpus enriched with synthetic math, code, and reasoning data (§3.1.1). Phi-4-Multimodal freezes this backbone and trains modality-specific LoRA adapters alongside vision and audio encoders under a Mixture-of-LoRAs design (§1, §2.2). A separate experimental reasoning variant is continued-pre-trained on about 60 billion chain-of-thought tokens, fine-tuned on roughly 200K curated CoT samples, then tuned by DPO on 300K preference pairs.
- **Key findings:** The abstract claims it significantly outperforms recent similar-size open models and matches models twice its size on math and coding, with Tables 7 and 8 benchmarking against the Llama-3.2, Qwen2.5, Ministral, Gemma, and Phi-3.5-Mini lines. Its speech LoRA, only 460M parameters, reportedly ranked first on the OpenASR leaderboard at publication time (Abstract). A responsible-AI section reports XSTest-derived refusal metrics separating inappropriate-prompt refusal (IPRR) from valid-prompt refusal (VPRR) in Table 12, and harmful-content defect rates averaged over four categories that improve on Phi-3.5-mini (Table 13).

## [8] A comprehensive evaluation of quantization strategies for large language models
- **Bibliography entry:** [8] R. Jin, J. Du, W. Huang, et al., “A comprehensive evaluation of quantization strategies for large language models,” in Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL), 2024. arXiv:2402.16775.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[08] Jin et al. - A comprehensive evaluation of quantization strategies for large language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: ACL 2024, pages 12186–12215 (venue footer visible in the extracted PDF text)
- **Problem & context:** Most quantization studies evaluate pre-trained rather than instruction-tuned LLMs, restrict testing to perplexity plus a few classification tasks, and leave unclear how reliably perplexity predicts downstream behaviour after quantization.
- **Approach:** The authors propose a structured evaluation across three dimensions, knowledge and capacity, alignment, and efficiency, using ten benchmarks including MMLU, C-Eval, GSM8K, TruthfulQA, BBQ, FollowBench, FLORES-200, SNLI, and two summarisation sets (Figure 1, Table 1). They quantize the instruction-tuned Qwen-Chat family at 7B, 14B, and 72B scales using LLM.int8(), GPTQ, and SpQR across bit widths from 2 to 8 bits (§3, Table 2), measuring memory consumption and decoding speed alongside task quality (§5).
- **Key findings:** Four-bit quantization keeps performance comparable to the non-quantized counterparts, while a notable discrepancy emerges once quantization drops to three bits or lower (Abstract, Conclusion). Perplexity serves as a reliable proxy for benchmark performance on most tasks. SpQR's isolation of outlier weights enables effective two-bit quantization that clearly outperforms GPTQ at the same level (Introduction). At similar memory cost the larger quantized model wins: Qwen-14B-Chat at 8-bit GPTQ needs memory comparable to Qwen-7B-Chat yet beats it on most benchmarks (§5, Figure 8a). Quantization frequently slows inference, with only the 72B model showing a significant speed-up at 4-bit (Figure 8b).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 1: Introduction > 1.1 Background and Motivation, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.2 Quantization Methods and Capability Effects

## [9] Evaluating quantized large language models
- **Bibliography entry:** [9] S. Li, X. Ning, L. Wang, T. Liu, X. Shi, S. Yan, G. Dai, H. Yang, and Y. Wang, “Evaluating quantized large language models,” in Proc. International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), 2024. arXiv:2402.18158.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[09] Li et al. - Evaluating quantized large language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Proceedings of the 41st International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML 2024), Vienna, PMLR 235 (venue footer visible in the extracted PDF text)
- **Problem & context:** Post-training quantization is standard practice, but earlier evaluations concentrated on language modelling and zero-shot classification, leaving dialogue, trustworthiness, emergent abilities, and long-context behaviour largely untested and offering practitioners little guidance on what to compress.
- **Approach:** The study evaluates quantization applied separately to weights, activations, and the KV cache over eleven model families (OPT, LLaMA2, Falcon, Bloomz, Mistral, ChatGLM, Vicuna, LongChat, StableLM, Gemma, Mamba) spanning 125M to 180B parameters, across five task types, additionally comparing SOTA methods such as AWQ and SmoothQuant (Abstract, §1). Dialogue quality is scored on MT-bench with GPT-4 single-answer grading (§6.1); trustworthiness covers ethics, hallucination, and adversarial robustness (§5.1).
- **Key findings:** Quantized-model rankings stay broadly consistent with FP16 above certain bit-widths (W4, W4A8, KV4); larger models tolerate weight-only and KV-cache quantization better while tolerating activation quantization less (Table 1). Table 1 also issues per-task bit-width recommendations calibrated to within 2% accuracy loss on the evaluated tasks. On ethics items, W3 models abandon the FP16 refusal behaviour and answer questions the full model declined, so measured accuracy paradoxically rises (§5.2, Figure 5). Longer inputs suffer more from weight and KV-cache quantization, and dialogue failures progress from sentence-level repetition to token-level randomness as bits shrink (Table 1, §6-§7).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 1: Introduction > 1.1 Background and Motivation, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.2 Quantization Methods and Capability Effects

## [10] Investigating the impact of quantization methods on the safety and reliability of large language models
- **Bibliography entry:** [10] A. Kharinaev, V. Moskvoretskii, E. Shvetsov et al., “Investigating the impact of quantization methods on the safety and reliability of large language models,” IEEE Access, vol. 14, pp. 96771–96793, 2026, doi: 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3703899. arXiv:2502.15799.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[10.1] Kharinaev et al. - Investigating the impact of quantization methods on the safety and reliability of large language models (IEEE Access version of record).pdf` (alternate version: `Sources/[10.2] Kharinaev et al. - ... (arXiv v2).pdf`)
- **Publication status:** Published in IEEE Access, vol. 14, 2026; the primary PDF shows DOI 10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3703899, publication date 15 June 2026, first page 96771, CC-BY license
- **Problem & context:** Quantization is widely deployed, yet its effect on safety remains poorly characterised because existing evaluations lean on multiple-choice or judge-scored suites that can misrepresent real unsafe behaviour, often using older architectures and datasets no longer challenging enough.
- **Approach:** The authors introduce OpenSafetyMini, a human-curated open-ended safety dataset of 1,067 prompts across eight risk categories, built by filtering low-ambiguity items with a model deflection score and labelling the remainder by five independent annotators under majority vote (Abstract, §I.A, §III). They release 4,268 human-labelled question-answer pairs and evaluate 66 quantized variants: the PTQ methods AWQ, QUIK, AQLM, and QUIP# at 8-, 4-, and 2-bit on LLaMA 3.1 8B Instruct, Mistral 7B Instruct v0.2, and Qwen2.5 at 1.5B, 7B, and 14B, plus QAT methods on LLaMA 3.1 8B (§IV.A-B). Four benchmarks combine OpenSafetyMini and XSafety open-ended testing, multiple-choice SafetyBench, and factuality scoring, judged by Gemma 2 27B at 92% agreement with humans (§IV.C).
- **Key findings:** Both PTQ and QAT degrade safety alignment, and weight-updating QAT does so more severely than PTQ, with extreme settings falling below even an abliterated LLaMA baseline whose refusal directions were removed (Contributions, §I). Closed-ended tests systematically misrepresent risk, hiding gaps of up to 35 percentage points that only open-ended evaluation reveals. For example, QUIK (93.21%) and AWQ (93.18%) are statistically indistinguishable on XSafety yet differ by 3.75 points on OpenSafetyMini (93.25% vs 89.50%) for LLaMA at 4-bit (§V). No method wins everywhere; precision-specialised methods excel at their target precision (QUIK/AWQ at 4-bit, AQLM/QUIP# at 2-bit). Human inter-rater agreement reached Krippendorff α of 0.623 (§IV.C).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 1: Introduction > 1.1 Background and Motivation

## [16] Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback
- **Bibliography entry:** [16] L. Ouyang et al., “Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback,” in Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS), vol. 35, 2022.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[23] Ouyang et al. - Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback.pdf`
- **Publication status:** 36th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2022) (venue footer visible in the extracted PDF text)
- **Problem & context:** Scaling alone does not make language models follow user intent; large LMs generate untruthful, toxic, or unhelpful outputs because next-token prediction diverges from the goal of following instructions helpfully, honestly, and harmlessly.
- **Approach:** OpenAI aligns GPT-3 through reinforcement learning from human feedback in three steps: supervised fine-tuning on labeler-written demonstrations, training a reward model on human-ranked output comparisons, and PPO optimisation against that reward (Figure 2). Around 40 contractor labelers supplied demonstrations and rankings, mostly on English API prompts, and models were trained at 1.3B, 6B, and 175B parameters on the GPT-3 architecture (§1, §3). A PPO-ptx variant mixes in pretraining-gradient updates to limit regressions on public NLP datasets (§1).
- **Key findings:** Outputs from the 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT model are preferred over outputs from 175B GPT-3 despite having 100x fewer parameters (Abstract, Figure 1); the 175B InstructGPT is preferred 85±3% of the time against 175B GPT-3 and 71±4% against few-shot prompting (§1). On closed-domain API tasks hallucination halves, 21% versus 41% (§1); toxic generations fall by about 25% when prompted to be respectful, while bias metrics show no significant improvement (§1). Held-out labelers prefer the models at similar rates, and FLAN/T0-style public-task fine-tuning underperforms RLHF on the API prompt distribution.
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.1 Trustworthiness and Safety Evaluation

## [21] Quantifying the capabilities of LLMs across scale and precision
- **Bibliography entry:** [21] S. Badshah and H. Sajjad, “Quantifying the capabilities of LLMs across scale and precision,” 2024. arXiv:2405.03146 (preprint).
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[29] Badshah et al. - Quantifying the capabilities of LLMs across scale and precision.pdf` (filename carries an earlier [29] numbering; extracted text is arXiv:2405.03146v2, dated 8 May 2024)
- **Publication status:** arXiv preprint (no archival venue shown)
- **Problem & context:** Resource constraints push users toward either smaller models or compressed large ones, but the joint effect of model scale and numerical precision on downstream task quality had not been thoroughly examined.
- **Approach:** Zero-shot evaluation of instruct models from two families, Llama 2-Chat (7B and 70B evaluated) together with Mistral 7B Instruct v0.2 and Mixtral 8x7B Instruct, each run at FP32, FP16, 8-bit via LLM.int8(), and 4-bit via QLoRA NF4, all implemented with bitsandbytes (§2.3-§2.5). Tasks cover natural language understanding (summarisation, machine translation, sentiment analysis), reasoning, misinformation detection, and hallucination, using 30 to 660 sampled test items per task; NLU is scored automatically while the remaining tasks use human evaluation (§2.1).
- **Key findings:** Larger models generally outperform their smaller counterparts, with exceptions: Mixtral 8x7B slightly trails Mistral 7B on SpartQA-hard spatial reasoning, and Mistral 7B tops all tested models on social-context tasks (Abstract, §1). Larger models show exceptional resilience to precision reduction, maintaining high accuracy even at 4-bit across numerous tasks (Abstract). Under a fixed memory budget the authors therefore recommend a larger quantized model: a 70B model at 4-bit, estimated at 42 GB (Table 1), consistently delivers superior performance compared with smaller high-precision alternatives (§1).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.2 Quantization Methods and Capability Effects

## [26] On calibration of modern neural networks
- **Bibliography entry:** [26] C. Guo, G. Pleiss, Y. Sun, and K. Q. Weinberger, “On calibration of modern neural networks,” in Proc. International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), PMLR vol. 70, pp. 1321–1330, 2017.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[34] Guo et al. - On calibration of modern neural networks.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Proceedings of the 34th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML 2017), Sydney, PMLR 70 (venue footer visible in the extracted PDF text)
- **Problem & context:** Deployed classifiers should signal when they are likely wrong, yet modern deep networks produce confidence estimates that do not represent true correctness likelihood, unlike networks from roughly a decade earlier (Abstract, §1).
- **Approach:** Using reliability diagrams plus expected and maximum calibration error, the study examines how depth, width, weight decay, and Batch Normalisation drive miscalibration on vision datasets such as CIFAR-100 and ImageNet and on NLP document classification, then compares post-hoc calibrators: histogram binning, isotonic regression, the binning-based BBQ, Platt scaling, and vector, matrix, and temperature scaling variants (§2-§4).
- **Key findings:** Deeper and wider networks are more miscalibrated despite lower error; Batch Normalisation worsens calibration, with a 6-layer ConvNet calibrating worse when BN is applied; and reduced weight decay hurts calibration (§3, Figures 2-3). Typical ECE values sit between 4% and 10% across datasets and architectures (§4). Temperature scaling, dividing logits by one learned parameter, leaves accuracy unchanged yet outperforms every other method on vision tasks and even beats the strictly more general vector and matrix scaling, dropping ResNet-110 CIFAR-100 ECE from 12.67 to 0.96 (Figure 4), supporting the claim that miscalibration is intrinsically low-dimensional.
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.2 Quantization Methods and Capability Effects

## [27] When quantization affects confidence of large language models?
- **Bibliography entry:** [27] I. Proskurina, L. Brun, G. Metzler, and J. Velcin, “When quantization affects confidence of large language models?,” in Findings of NAACL, 2024. arXiv:2405.00632.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[11] Proskurina et al. - When quantization affects confidence of large language models.pdf` (filename carries an earlier [11] numbering; extracted text shows pages 1918–1928)
- **Publication status:** Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: NAACL 2024, pages 1918–1928 (venue footer visible in the extracted PDF text)
- **Problem & context:** Quantized LLMs cut storage and latency, but whether compression preserves not only accuracy but also well-calibrated confidence, and why losses vary across inputs, had received little systematic attention (§1).
- **Approach:** The study applies GPTQ 4-bit quantisation, group size 128 and calibrated on 128 sampled C4 sequences (§3.1), to BLOOM-7.1B, OPT-6.7B, Mistral-7B, and LLaMA-7B, with additional analysis across OPT and BLOOM scales down to hundreds of millions of parameters, comparing pre- and post-compression behaviour zero-shot over six commonsense benchmarks: ARC-Easy, BoolQ, PIQA, HellaSwag, OpenBookQA, and XStory-EN (§3.2, §4). Metrics include accuracy, expected and adaptive calibration error, mean confidence on true versus erroneous predictions, entropy, and Jensen-Shannon distances between full and compressed output distributions.
- **Key findings:** Quantisation lowers confidence in true labels, with effects varying by model family and scale (Abstract). Calibration error generally grows after compression, most sharply for LLaMA-7B, whose HellaSwag calibration error rises by roughly 10% relative to before compression (Calibration Impact discussion, Table 1). Models remain overconfident both before and after compression, averaging about 0.95 confidence even on incorrect predictions (Table 2 discussion), and confidence shifts concentrate on samples the uncompressed model was already unsure about (Figure 1), while distributional distance to the full model shrinks as size grows (Figure 2).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.2 Quantization Methods and Capability Effects

## [28] How does quantization affect multilingual LLMs?
- **Bibliography entry:** [28] K. Marchisio, S. Dash, H. Chen, D. Aumiller, A. Üstün, S. Hooker, and S. Ruder, “How does quantization affect multilingual LLMs?,” in Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EMNLP 2024, pp. 15928–15947, 2024. arXiv:2407.03211.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[13] Marchisio et al. - How does quantization affect multilingual LLMs.pdf` (filename carries an earlier [13] numbering; extracted text shows pages 15928–15947)
- **Publication status:** Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EMNLP 2024, pages 15928–15947 (venue footer visible in the extracted PDF text)
- **Problem & context:** Quantization research is overwhelmingly English-only, so the effect of compression on multilingual generation had not been characterised, although underserved languages and severe compute constraints often geographically co-occur (§1).
- **Approach:** The authors analyse four state-of-the-art multilingual LLMs across three sizes from 8 to 103 billion parameters covering up to 23 languages, namely Command R+ (103B), Command R (35B), and Aya 23 (35B, 8B) (§1, §3). Quantization configurations include W8A8, W8A8 with SmoothQuant, W4 with column-wise scaling, and W4 group-wise GPTQ for the Command models, plus bitsandbytes 8-bit and 4-bit for Aya 23, calibrated on 128 English samples (§3). Evaluation combines automatic suites (multilingual MMLU, MGSM, FLORES-200, language confusion), LLM-as-a-Judge, and multilingual human evaluation on realistic prompts.
- **Key findings:** Automatic metrics severely underestimate damage: French and Japanese average drops of −0.3% and −1.7% automatically become −16.6% and −16.0% under human evaluation (Findings 1, Figure 1). Languages are disparately affected, non-Latin script languages worst, −1.9% versus −0.7% for Latin-script at 103B parameters (Findings 2). Challenging tasks degrade fastest: mathematical reasoning −13.1%, human-judged real-world prompts −10.5%, LLM-as-a-Judge −25.9% (Findings 3). Occasionally quantization helps multilingually, for instance a +1.3% average boost for a 35B W8A8 model (Findings 4).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.2 Quantization Methods and Capability Effects
