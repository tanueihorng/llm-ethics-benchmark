## [11] XSTest: A test suite for identifying exaggerated safety behaviours in large language models
- **Bibliography entry:** [11] P. Röttger, H. R. Kirk, B. Vidgen, G. Attanasio, F. Bianchi, and D. Hovy, "XSTest: A test suite for identifying exaggerated safety behaviours in large language models," in Proc. NAACL, 2024. arXiv:2308.01263.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[18] Röttger et al. - XSTest - A test suite for identifying exaggerated safety behaviours in large language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed venue shown in the PDF: Proceedings of NAACL 2024, Human Language Technologies, Volume 1: Long Papers, pages 5377-5400 (ACL copyright line present).
- **Problem & context:** Safety training creates a tension between harmlessness and helpfulness: models must refuse unsafe prompts but should still answer safe ones. The authors build on anecdotal evidence that Llama 2 refused clearly safe prompts whose wording resembled unsafe content, a false-refusal failure mode they call exaggerated safety, which prior safety datasets largely ignored.
- **Approach:** XSTest is a hand-crafted diagnostic suite of 250 safe prompts across ten types designed to superficially resemble unsafe requests (homonyms, figurative language, safe targets, safe contexts, definitions, discrimination counterfactuals, historical events, public and fictional privacy), plus 200 minimally edited unsafe contrasts (§3). Five configurations are tested: Llama-2-70b-chat with and without its system prompt, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1 with and without a guardrail prompt, and GPT-4. All responses are manually annotated as full compliance, full refusal, or partial refusal by three annotators, two per prompt, with agreement between 93.8 and 98.4 percent (Fleiss' kappa 0.89 to 0.97) (§4.2); string matching and a GPT-4 judge are compared against manual labels as automated alternatives (§4.5).
- **Key findings:** On safe prompts, Llama2.0 fully refuses 38 percent and partially refuses another 21.6 percent; removing the system prompt lowers this to 14 and 15.6 percent (Table 1). Mistral without a guardrail shows 0.8 full and 0.8 partial refusal but complies with the majority of unsafe prompts; adding the guardrail gives 9.6 and 9.2 percent (Table 1, §4.4). GPT-4 records 6.4 and 2 percent, concentrated in fictional privacy where it fully refuses 52 percent of T10 prompts (Table 1, §4.3). Both Llama 2 variants refuse all 200 unsafe prompts; GPT-4 refuses all but one (§4.4). The authors attribute exaggerated safety to lexical overfitting (§1).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 1: Introduction > 1.1 Background and Motivation, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.4 Refusal, Harmfulness, and Threat Models

## [12] HarmBench: A standardized evaluation framework for automated red teaming and robust refusal
- **Bibliography entry:** [12] M. Mazeika et al., "HarmBench: A standardized evaluation framework for automated red teaming and robust refusal," in Proc. International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), 2024. arXiv:2402.04249.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[24.1] Mazeika et al. - HarmBench - A standardized evaluation framework for automated red teaming and robust refusal (PMLR v235 version of record).pdf` (an alternate author camera-ready file, `Sources/[24.2] ... (author camera-ready).pdf`, also exists in the repo; the PMLR version of record was extracted)
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed venue shown in the PDF: Proceedings of the 41st International Conference on Machine Learning, Vienna, Austria, PMLR 235, 2024.
- **Problem & context:** Automated red teaming methods had been developed and evaluated under disparate, rarely overlapping pipelines, so their relative performance was unknown. The paper argues the field lacked a standardized framework with explicit desirable properties for comparing attacks and defenses on harmful-behavior elicitation.
- **Approach:** HarmBench is designed for breadth, comparability, and robust metrics. It contains 510 curated harmful behaviors, 400 textual and 110 multimodal, in four functional categories (200 standard, 100 copyright, 100 contextual, 110 multimodal) and seven semantic categories, with an official validation/test split of 100 and 410 behaviors (Figure 1 caption, §4.2). Attack success rate is computed with a classifier fine-tuned from Llama 2 13B Chat, whose agreement with human judgments is comparable to GPT-4 (§4.2 Classifiers, Table 4). Using this setup the authors compare 18 red teaming methods against 33 target LLMs and defenses and propose R2D2, an efficient adversarial training method for robust refusal (Abstract, §5.2).
- **Key findings:** No current attack or defense is uniformly effective, and robustness is independent of model size across six families spanning 7 to 70 billion parameters, suggesting training procedures matter more than scale (§5.1, Figure 6). ASR is considerably higher on contextual behaviors and relatively low on copyright behaviors because the hashing-based copyright classifier applies a stricter standard (§5.1). Zephyr 7B trained with R2D2 achieves state-of-the-art robustness against GCG among model-level defenses, cutting ASR from 31.8 to 5.9 percent versus Llama 2 7B Chat and from 30.2 to 5.9 versus Llama 2 13B Chat, while keeping MT-Bench at 6.0 versus Mistral 7B Instruct v0.2's 6.5 (§5.2).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 1: Introduction > 1.1 Background and Motivation, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.1 Trustworthiness and Safety Evaluation

## [29] HarmLevelBench: Evaluating harm-level compliance and the impact of quantization on model alignment
- **Bibliography entry:** [29] Y. Belkhiter, G. Zizzo, and S. Maffeis, "HarmLevelBench: Evaluating harm-level compliance and the impact of quantization on model alignment," arXiv:2411.06835, Nov. 2024. Presented at the NeurIPS 2024 Workshop on Safe Generative AI (SafeGenAI), which is non-archival and issues no proceedings.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[35] Belkhiter et al. - HarmLevelBench - Evaluating harm-level compliance and the impact of quantization on model alignment.pdf`
- **Publication status:** arXiv preprint (no archival venue shown); the PDF header notes presentation at the NeurIPS 2024 Workshop on Safe Generative Artificial Intelligence (SafeGenAI), which the bibliography entry itself flags as non-archival with no proceedings.
- **Problem & context:** Existing jailbreak datasets lack structured query formulation and any graded notion of how harmful a query is, limiting fine-grained analysis of compliance, while the effect of quantization on alignment remained underexplored.
- **Approach:** HarmLevelBench covers seven harmful topics with eight severity levels each under a uniform question template, and the paper benchmarks seven jailbreaking techniques of increasing complexity (Simple Query, Ignorance Context, Role Play, PAP, PAIR, AutoDAN-GA, GCG) against Vicuna 13B v1.5, scoring attack success by human annotation, string detection, and a GPT judge (§2 to §3). The pipeline is then applied to AWQ and GPTQ quantized versions of the same model under direct attacks, and to transferred attacks crafted on the original model (§4.3, §4.4).
- **Key findings:** On the original model, complex attacks approach saturation, with AutoDAN reaching human ASR 100 and GCG string ASR 100 while Simple Query attains 33.9 (Table 2). Query harmfulness mainly reduces success for low and moderate complexity jailbreaks (§4.2). Direct attacks on the AWQ model yield higher ASR for query-based attacks, with PAIR string ASR 98.2, but lower success for advanced attacks, GCG human ASR falling to 66.1 from 92.9 (Table 3, §4.3). Transferred attacks weaken sharply on both quantized copies, PAIR dropping from 94.6 to 66.1 (AWQ) and 64.3 (GPTQ), and AutoDAN from 100 to 71.4 and 67.9, supporting the claimed trade-off between enhanced transfer-attack robustness and increased direct-attack vulnerability (§4.4, Table 4).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.3 Quantization and Safety Behaviour

## [30] Assessing safety risks and quantization-aware safety patching for quantized large language models
- **Bibliography entry:** [30] K. Chen, J. Zhang, J. Hu et al., "Assessing safety risks and quantization-aware safety patching for quantized large language models," in Proc. International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), PMLR vol. 267, pp. 9728–9746, 2025. arXiv:2506.20251.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[36] Chen et al. - Assessing safety risks and quantization-aware safety patching for quantized large language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed venue shown in the PDF: Proceedings of the 42nd International Conference on Machine Learning, Vancouver, Canada, PMLR 267, 2025.
- **Problem & context:** Emerging studies suggested that some calibration-dataset-free quantization methods compromise LLM safety, motivating a systematic evaluation across mainstream techniques and calibration datasets, together with an efficient mitigation.
- **Approach:** The assessment covers four representative methods, PTQ via AWQ and AQLM and QAT via LLM-QAT and QLoRA, at INT8 and INT4 with 2-bit and 3-bit ablations, on Llama-2-7B-Chat and Gemma-7B-Instruct. Three ten-sample quantization-assisting datasets of varying risk (benign UltraChat, indirectly harmful, directly harmful, the latter two crafted from AdvBench) are compared, with safety measured by AdvBench ASR and utility by MT-Bench and AlpacaEval (§3.1, Tables 2 and 3). For mitigation, Q-resafe builds a safety-patching dataset guided by the pre-quantization model and updates only identified safety-critical weights through a DPO-style objective (§4).
- **Key findings:** All evaluated techniques degrade safety, harmful calibration samples trigger dramatic drops, and lower bit-widths degrade more than higher ones (contributions list, §1). Relative to the pre-quantization models, baseline methods raise ASR by up to 92.3 percent for Llama and 66.7 percent for Gemma under the direct-harm setting while Q-resafe adds only 13.6 and 1.8; under the benign setting increases are 16.6 and 11.5 versus 1.5 and 0.9; under indirect harm 82.6 and 59.2 versus 13.3 and 5.5; in the fine-tuning-free AWQ comparison, AWQ adds up to 7.3 and 5.8 versus 0.8 and 0.4 for Q-resafe (§5.1). Utility remains close to the pre-quantization models throughout.
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.3 Quantization and Safety Behaviour

## [31] Decoding compressed trust: Scrutinizing the trustworthiness of efficient LLMs under compression
- **Bibliography entry:** [31] J. Hong, J. Duan, C. Zhang, Z. Li, C. Xie, K. Lieberman, J. Diffenderfer, B. R. Bartoldson, A. K. Jaiswal, K. Xu, B. Kailkhura, D. Hendrycks, D. Song, Z. Wang, and B. Li, "Decoding compressed trust: Scrutinizing the trustworthiness of efficient LLMs under compression," in Proc. International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), PMLR vol. 235, 2024. arXiv:2403.15447.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[37] Hong et al. - Decoding compressed trust - Scrutinizing the trustworthiness of efficient LLMs under compression.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed venue shown in the PDF: Proceedings of the 41st International Conference on Machine Learning, Vienna, Austria, PMLR 235, 2024.
- **Problem & context:** Compression is the default route to resource-efficient LLM inference, but prior evaluations concentrated on benign utility, leaving the trustworthiness consequences of compression largely unexamined.
- **Approach:** The study evaluates three 13B models (LLaMA2 13b, LLaMA2 13b Chat, Vicuna 13b Chat) compressed by five techniques: Magnitude, SparseGPT, and Wanda pruning at 2:4 sparsity, plus GPTQ and AWQ quantization at 3, 4, and 8 bits (Abstract, Table 1). Trustworthiness is scored on eight DecodingTrust dimensions (stereotype, privacy, toxicity, fairness, ethics, adversarial robustness, OOD robustness, robustness to adversarial demonstrations) alongside MMLU utility and refusal rates (§3). It also compares pre-training small models against compressing larger ones and probes extreme 3-bit settings (§4, §5).
- **Key findings:** Quantization is more effective than pruning at combining efficiency and trustworthiness; a 4-bit quantized model retains the trustworthiness of its original while pruning degrades it even at 50 percent sparsity, and moderate bit ranges can improve some dimensions such as ethics and fairness (Abstract). The recommended operating point is 4-bit for LLaMA2 Chat 13b with less than five points loss on all dimensions, whereas at 3 bits GPTQ degrades over fifty points in the worst case, including roughly thirty-point OOD and fifty-point toxicity drops, and an adversary could exploit GPTQ to break alignment at about an eight-point MMLU cost (§5.2 takeaways). These risks cannot be uncovered from benign performance alone.
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.3 Quantization and Safety Behaviour

## [33] Durable quantization conditioned misalignment attack on large language models
- **Bibliography entry:** [33] P. Dong, H. Li, and S. Guo, "Durable quantization conditioned misalignment attack on large language models," in Proc. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR), 2025.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[39] Dong et al. - Durable quantization conditioned misalignment attack on large language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed venue shown in the PDF: banner "Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2025".
- **Problem & context:** Users increasingly download open-source LLMs and quantize them locally for edge deployment. The paper defines a threat in which safety misalignment lies dormant in a full-precision checkpoint but becomes exploitable after quantization, and must survive downstream customization by unsuspecting users.
- **Approach:** The Q-Misalign attack runs in two phases: fine-tuning a pretrained model into an explicitly harmful one, then a constrained unlearning stage that removes harmful behavior at full precision while a PGD-based loss keeps the quantized weights aligned with their harmful counterparts (§4.1). A contrastive task vector variant clusters parameters by their relative change under harmful versus benign tuning and attacks only safety-related ones, so the misalignment persists through later fine-tuning (§4.2). Experiments cover InternLM2-Chat-1.8b, Gemma-1.1-2b-it, and Llama-2-7b-chat under INT8, NF4, and FP4 quantization, scoring jailbreak success on AdvBench and utility on TruthfulQA MC2 (§5.1).
- **Key findings:** After phase two, the implicit harmful model looks safe at full precision (ASR 0.03 for InternLM2-Chat-1.8b) but quantization raises ASR to 0.94 under INT8, 0.94 under NF4, and 0.93 under FP4, exceeding 90 percent across models (Table 1, §5.2). Post-quantization utility loss shrinks with scale, about 8 percent for the 1.8b model versus 4 percent at 7b (§5.2). The misalignment is durable: after one epoch of supervised fine-tuning on Alpaca, quantized ASR remains 0.78 for Q-Misalign versus 0.44 for the ELQ baseline of Egashira et al. on InternLM2-Chat-1.8b (Table 4).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.3 Quantization and Safety Behaviour, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.6 Synthesis and Research Gap

## [34] Safety layers in aligned large language models: The key to LLM security
- **Bibliography entry:** [34] S. Li, L. Yao, L. Zhang, and Y. Li, "Safety layers in aligned large language models: The key to LLM security," in Proc. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR), 2025.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[40] Li et al. - Safety layers in aligned large language models - The key to LLM security.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed venue shown in the PDF: banner "Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2025".
- **Problem & context:** Aligned LLMs recognize and refuse malicious questions, but which internal parameters carry that ability was not understood, and fine-tuning attacks can silently destroy it, so defenses lacked a parameter-level account of where security lives.
- **Approach:** Working with four aligned models (Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Llama-2-7b-chat, gemma-2b-it, Phi-3-mini-4k-instruct), the authors compare layer-wise cosine similarity of final-position hidden vectors for normal-normal versus normal-malicious query pairs, finding a gap that emerges only in middle layers and is absent in pre-trained checkpoints, evidence that safety alignment itself creates the "safety layers" (§3.2, §3.3). Exact layer bounds are localized by scaling layer parameters and reading the resulting over-rejection behavior (§3.4). The proposed SPPFT method freezes the gradients of the localized safety layers during fine-tuning, evaluated against full fine-tuning, neuron freezing, and Lisa across normal, implicit-harm, and backdoor scenarios using a 520-prompt malicious set scored by harmful rate and a GPT-4 harmful score (§4).
- **Key findings:** Across the three scenarios, full fine-tuning raises the harmful rate by up to 58.03 percentage points (implicit scenario) while SPPFT limits increases to between 2.84 and 4.42 points, with harmful score rises of 0.10 to 0.18 versus up to 2.17 (§4.3, Table 2). Rouge-L and MMLU change minimally under SPPFT, and freezing the discrete security-critical neurons of Wei et al. fails to prevent degradation, indicating the contiguous layers are what matter (§4.3).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.3 Quantization and Safety Behaviour

## [35] Refusal in language models is mediated by a single direction
- **Bibliography entry:** [35] A. Arditi, O. Obeso, A. Syed, D. Paleka, N. Panickssery, W. Gurnee, and N. Nanda, "Refusal in language models is mediated by a single direction," in Proc. NeurIPS, 2024. arXiv:2406.11717.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[15] Arditi et al. - Refusal in language models is mediated by a single direction.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed venue shown in the PDF: footer "38th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2024)".
- **Problem & context:** Chat models refuse harmful instructions after safety fine-tuning, but the mechanism underlying refusal was poorly understood, limiting both the analysis of jailbreaks and deliberate behavioral control.
- **Approach:** Across 13 open-source chat models from five families spanning 1.8B to 72B parameters, the authors extract a single difference-in-means direction between mean activations on 128 harmful instructions (from AdvBench, MaliciousInstruct, TDC2023, and HarmBench) and 128 harmless Alpaca instructions (§2.2, §2.3). Erasing this direction from residual-stream activations suppresses refusal, while adding it elicits refusal on harmless prompts. The insight is converted into a white-box jailbreak that orthogonally projects out the direction from every weight matrix writing to the residual stream, followed by an analysis of how adversarial suffixes interfere with propagation of the direction (Abstract, §4).
- **Key findings:** Evaluated on the HarmBench test set of 159 standard behaviors with the official classifier, the weight-editing method reaches ASR 22.6 percent on Llama-2 7B with the default system prompt and 79.9 percent without it, and 78.0 to 84.3 percent on Qwen models regardless of system prompt, performing on par with prompt-specific GCG within the Qwen family (Table 2, §4.2). Orthogonalized models keep MMLU, ARC, and GSM8K scores close to baseline; apart from Qwen 7B and Yi 34B all metrics lie within 99 percent confidence intervals, though TruthfulQA accuracy drops consistently (§4.3, Table 3). The authors read the results as showing the brittleness of current safety fine-tuning.
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.3 Quantization and Safety Behaviour

## [36] Safety alignment should be made more than just a few tokens deep
- **Bibliography entry:** [36] X. Qi, A. Panda, K. Lyu, X. Ma, S. Roy, A. Beirami, P. Mittal, and P. Henderson, "Safety alignment should be made more than just a few tokens deep," in Proc. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR), 2025. arXiv:2406.05946.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[16] Qi et al. - Safety alignment should be made more than just a few tokens deep.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed venue shown in the PDF: banner "Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2025".
- **Problem & context:** Aligned LLMs remain vulnerable to simple attacks and even benign fine-tuning. The paper argues many vulnerabilities share one root cause, shallow safety alignment, in which alignment reshapes the generative distribution almost entirely over the first few output tokens.
- **Approach:** Case studies characterize the issue: Llama-2-7B-Chat begins responses with "I cannot" or "I apologize" in 96.1 percent of HEx-PHI instances, and per-token KL divergence between aligned and base models concentrates on the earliest tokens (§2.2, Figure 1). As a counterfactual, safety recovery examples (synthetic triplets training a refusal to resume after harmful prefixes) deepen the alignment of Llama-2-7B-Chat (§3.1). A token-wise constrained fine-tuning objective additionally downweights updates wherever the distribution drifts from the aligned model, with the strongest constraints on the first five tokens (§4.1).
- **Key findings:** Prefilling growing numbers of harmful tokens pushes aligned-model ASR from near zero to over 50 percent, and fine-tuning on 100 harmful examples lifts ASR from 1.5 to 22.4 after two gradient steps and 87.9 after six (Figure 2, Figure 3 note). The augmented model cuts prefilling ASR at five prefilled tokens from 42.1 to 2.8, GCG ASR on HEx-PHI from 36.5 to 18.4, and decoding-parameter exploit ASR from 54.9 to 11.3, with utility preserved (Tables 2 and 3, §3.2). The constrained objective holds ASR to 4.6 versus 88.9 for standard SFT under the harmful-data attack, and during benign Samsum fine-tuning keeps ASR at 3.2 versus 23.4 with near-identical utility, 50.1 versus 51.7 (Table 4, §4.2).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.3 Quantization and Safety Behaviour

## [37] Exploiting LLM quantization
- **Bibliography entry:** [37] K. Egashira, M. Vero, R. Staab, J. He, and M. Vechev, "Exploiting LLM quantization," in Proc. NeurIPS, 2024. arXiv:2405.18137.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[12] Egashira et al. - Exploiting LLM quantization.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed venue shown in the PDF: footer "38th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2024)".
- **Problem & context:** Weight quantization is how users deploy large open models on commodity hardware, yet its security implications had not been studied. The paper asks whether a distributed full-precision model can appear benign while its quantized derivatives behave maliciously.
- **Approach:** A three-stage framework targets zero-shot methods (LLM.int8(), NF4, FP4): instruction-tune a malicious model, derive interval constraints characterizing all full-precision weights that quantize to that malicious model, then use projected gradient descent to tune the malicious behavior out of the full-precision copy while staying inside the constraints (§3). Attacks run on StarCoder-1b, StarCoder-3b, StarCoder-7b, Phi-2, and Gemma-2b across three scenarios, vulnerable code generation via reversed SafeCoder tuning, over-refusal, and content injection, with utility tracked on MMLU, TruthfulQA, HumanEval, and MBPP (§4). A Gaussian noise-based defense is also studied (§1 contributions, §4.4).
- **Key findings:** In code generation, the attacked StarCoder-1b keeps a code security rate of 82.6 percent at FP32 but drops to 2.8 under LLM.int8(), 5.6 under NF4, and 7.2 under FP4, and attacked models produce vulnerable code up to 97.2 percent of the time (Table 1). In the over-refusal scenario, original and attacked full-precision models refuse fewer than 2.3 percent of 1,500 benign instructions while quantized copies refuse up to 39.1 percent (Table 2, §4.2). Content injection reaches keyword occurrence up to 74.7 percent after quantization (Table 3). Utility metrics remain close to the original models throughout.
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.3 Quantization and Safety Behaviour, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.6 Synthesis and Research Gap
