## [19] Measuring Massive Multitask Language Understanding
- **Bibliography entry:** [19] D. Hendrycks, C. Burns, S. Basart, A. Zou, M. Mazeika, D. Song, and J. Steinhardt, “Measuring massive multitask language understanding,” in Proc. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR), 2021. arXiv:2009.03300.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[27] Hendrycks et al. - Measuring massive multitask language understanding.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed conference paper, ICLR 2021 (header reads "Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2021").
- **Problem & context:** Prior NLP benchmarks such as GLUE and SuperGLUE are saturated quickly and measure linguistic skill more than broad world knowledge, so they fail to reveal what models actually learned during pretraining. The authors want a durable test of academic and professional knowledge across many subjects.
- **Approach:** They build a multiple-choice test of 15,908 questions covering 57 subjects across STEM, humanities, social science and other areas, from practice exams such as the GRE and USMLE (§3). Splits are a 5-per-subject dev set, a 1,540-question validation set and a 14,079-question test set (§3). Evaluation is zero-shot and few-shot only, reading the model's probability over the answer tokens (§4.1). Models evaluated include four GPT-3 sizes (2.7B to 175B), UnifiedQA on a T5 backbone, and fine-tuned RoBERTa, ALBERT and GPT-2 (§4.1).
- **Key findings:** The 175B GPT-3 reaches 43.9% few-shot accuracy versus roughly 25% random chance, while its three smaller sizes stay near chance (Abstract, Table 1, §4.2); its zero-shot accuracy is about 37.7% (§4.2). The largest UnifiedQA attains 48.9% despite an order of magnitude fewer parameters (Table 1, §4.2). Unspecialized humans score 34.5% and expert-level accuracy is estimated at 89.8%, which no model approaches on any task (§3). Performance is lopsided, from 69% on US Foreign Policy to 26% on College Chemistry, with morality and law tasks near random (§4.2). Confidence deviates from accuracy by up to 24% zero-shot (Figure 8, §4.2).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.1 Trustworthiness and Safety Evaluation

## [20] Think you have Solved Question Answering? Try ARC, the AI2 Reasoning Challenge
- **Bibliography entry:** [20] P. Clark, I. Cowhey, O. Etzioni, T. Khot, A. Sabharwal, C. Schoenick, and O. Tafjord, “Think you have solved question answering? Try ARC, the AI2 reasoning challenge,” arXiv:1803.05457, 2018 (preprint).
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[28] Clark et al. - Think you have solved question answering Try ARC, the AI2 reasoning challenge.pdf`
- **Publication status:** arXiv preprint (no archival venue shown); the PDF shows only the arXiv stamp (arXiv:1803.05457v1, 14 Mar 2018) and the AI2 affiliation.
- **Problem & context:** Existing QA datasets reward retrieval-style surface cues, so reported progress concentrates on easy factoid questions while reasoning remains untested. Standardized science exams would probe deeper skill but public question sets of this kind were scarce.
- **Approach:** The authors assemble 7,787 non-diagram multiple-choice grade-school science questions from 3rd to 9th grade standardized tests, split into a Challenge Set of 2,590 questions answered incorrectly by both an information-retrieval solver over a large web corpus and a word co-occurrence (PMI) solver, and an Easy Set of 5,197 questions (Introduction, §The ARC Dataset, Tables 1-3). They release the ARC Corpus of 14M science sentences and adapt neural baselines (DecompAttn, DGEM, BiDAF) plus TableILP and TupleInference to the task (§Baseline Performance).
- **Key findings:** On the Challenge Set no system significantly beats the random baseline of 25.02% (95% CI ±2.5%); the best score is DGEM at 27.11%, with BiDAF at 26.54% (Table 6, Results). On the Easy Set the same baselines generally score between 55% and 65%, showing the Challenge partition is qualitatively harder (Results, Table 6). The corpus mentions knowledge relevant to about 95% of Challenge questions, but simple retrieval fails to exploit it, indicating the need for multi-fact combination (§The ARC Corpus).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.1 Trustworthiness and Safety Evaluation

## [42] OR-Bench: An Over-Refusal Benchmark for Large Language Models
- **Bibliography entry:** [42] J. Cui, W.-L. Chiang, I. Stoica, and C.-J. Hsieh, “OR-Bench: An over-refusal benchmark for large language models,” in Proc. International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), 2025. arXiv:2405.20947.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[19] Cui et al. - OR-Bench - An over-refusal benchmark for large language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed, ICML 2025 (footer reads "Proceedings of the 42nd International Conference on Machine Learning, Vancouver, Canada. PMLR 267, 2025").
- **Problem & context:** Safety alignment pushes LLMs to refuse innocuous prompts, and the only existing dedicated test, XSTest, has 250 hand-written prompts that newer models already answer almost fully, so over-refusal cannot be measured at scale.
- **Approach:** An automated pipeline uses Mixtral 8x7B to generate toxic seeds across ten rejection categories and rewrite them into borderline benign prompts, then filters them with a majority-vote ensemble moderator of GPT-4-turbo-2024-04-09, Llama-3-70b and Gemini-1.5-pro-latest (§3.2). This yields OR-Bench-80K of 80,000 safe prompts prone to refusal, a harder OR-Bench-Hard-1K subset, and a 600-prompt toxic set; 32 models from 8 families are evaluated at temperature 0 with keyword matching plus GPT-4 checks (Abstract, §4.1).
- **Key findings:** The Spearman rank correlation between toxic-prompt rejection and over-refusal is 0.89, meaning most models trade over-refusal for safety (Figure 1 caption, §4.2). Over-refusal on the full 80K reaches 49% for GPT-3.5-turbo-0301 and 73% for Claude-2.1 (§3.3). GPT-3.5-turbo-0301 rejects over 57% of Hard-1K, fixed in later releases, but GPT-3.5-turbo-0125 then answers only 62% of toxic prompts (§4.2). Claude models show the highest safety with the most over-refusal while Mistral accepts most prompts (§1). The ensemble moderator reaches over 98% of human-expert performance (§4.1, Table 1).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.4 Refusal, Harmfulness, and Threat Models

## [43] Do-Not-Answer: Evaluating Safeguards in LLMs
- **Bibliography entry:** [43] Y. Wang, H. Li, X. Han, P. Nakov, and T. Baldwin, “Do-Not-Answer: Evaluating safeguards in LLMs,” in Findings of EACL, pp. 896–911, 2024, doi: 10.18653/v1/2024.findings-eacl.61.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[45] Wang et al. - Do-Not-Answer - Evaluating safeguards in LLMs.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed, Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EACL 2024, pages 896–911 (running header).
- **Problem & context:** Open-source LLMs largely lack the safety mechanisms of commercial systems, and developers need a low-cost way to evaluate whether models appropriately decline risky instructions.
- **Approach:** The authors release an open dataset of 939 instructions that responsible models should not follow, generated with GPT-4 through fabricated chat histories under a three-level risk taxonomy of five top-level areas, twelve harm types and 61 specific risks extending Weidinger et al. (§1-3, Table 1). They collect 5,634 responses from six LLMs (GPT-4, ChatGPT, Claude v1, Vicuna-7B, LLaMA-2-7B-chat, ChatGLM2-7B), annotate them into six action categories where category 5 means direct harmful compliance, and compare GPT-4 prompting with a fine-tuned Longformer classifier under 6-fold cross-validation as automatic evaluators (§3.2, §4, §5).
- **Key findings:** Human annotation finds LLaMA-2 safest with only 3 harmful responses out of 939, then ChatGPT 14, Claude 16, GPT-4 23, Vicuna 52 and ChatGLM2 85 (§4.2.1). Both evaluators perform strongly on binary harmful-response detection with over 98% accuracy and 80% macro-F1, Longformer averaging 98.1±1.2 accuracy versus GPT-4's 98.4±1.4 (Table 4, §5.3). The evaluators reproduce the human safety ranking almost exactly, differing only in the order of ChatGPT and Claude (§5.3).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.4 Refusal, Harmfulness, and Threat Models, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.5 Automatic Evaluators and Human Validation, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.6 Synthesis and Research Gap

## [44] Prometheus 2: An Open Source Language Model Specialized in Evaluating Other Language Models
- **Bibliography entry:** [44] S. Kim et al., “Prometheus 2: An open source language model specialized in evaluating other language models,” in Proc. EMNLP, pp. 4334–4353, 2024, doi: 10.18653/v1/2024.emnlp-main.248.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[46] Kim et al. - Prometheus 2 - An open source language model specialized in evaluating other language models.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed, Proceedings of EMNLP 2024 (main conference), pages 4334–4353 (footer).
- **Problem & context:** Proprietary judges such as GPT-4 dominate LLM evaluation but raise transparency, controllability and affordability problems, while existing open evaluator LMs correlate poorly with humans and usually support either direct assessment or pairwise ranking, not both.
- **Approach:** The authors train separate evaluator LMs on Mistral-7B and Mixtral-8x7B for direct assessment (1-5 scoring with rubrics and reference answers) and for pairwise ranking, then merge their weights into unified Prometheus 2 models, finding merging beats joint or single-format training (§1, §3, §6). Training data includes the new Preference Collection built from the Feedback Collection, with 1,000 custom evaluation criteria, 200K pairwise instances and 100K direct-assessment instances (Table 1). Evaluation covers four direct assessment benchmarks (Vicuna Bench, MT Bench, FLASK, Feedback Bench) and four ranking benchmarks (HHH Alignment, MT Bench Human Judgment, Auto-J Eval, Preference Bench) against open and proprietary baselines (§5).
- **Key findings:** Prometheus 2 correlates with humans, GPT-4-1106 and Claude-3-Opus at Pearson values above 0.5 on every direct assessment benchmark, while base, single-format and jointly trained LMs mostly stay below 0.5 (§5.1). It exceeds Prometheus and Auto-J by at least 0.2 correlation units against proprietary judges (§5.1); on FLASK it reaches 0.555 with humans versus 0.449 for Prometheus-13B, against a human-GPT-4 value of 0.679 (§5.1). On pairwise ranking it posts the highest agreement among open evaluators, halving the gap to GPT-4 (Abstract, Table 4, §5.2).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.5 Automatic Evaluators and Human Validation, Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.6 Synthesis and Research Gap

## [45] Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena
- **Bibliography entry:** [45] L. Zheng et al., “Judging LLM-as-a-judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena,” in Proc. NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks Track, 2023. arXiv:2306.05685.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[47] Zheng et al. - Judging LLM-as-a-judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed, NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track (footer reads "37th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2023) Track on Datasets and Benchmarks").
- **Problem & context:** Conventional capability benchmarks do not capture alignment with human preferences on open-ended chat, and collecting human preference labels is slow and costly, motivating strong LLMs as surrogate judges.
- **Approach:** The paper introduces MT-bench, 80 two-turn questions across eight categories (writing, roleplay, extraction, reasoning, math, coding, STEM and humanities knowledge), evaluated with 58 expert labelers producing about 3K votes over six models, and Chatbot Arena, a crowdsourced anonymous battle platform that gathered about 30K votes in one month (§2, §4.1). Three judge modes are studied: pairwise comparison, single answer grading and reference-guided grading, alongside analyses of position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases (§3).
- **Key findings:** GPT-4 as judge agrees with human experts on 85% of non-tied MT-bench votes, above the 81% human-human agreement (§4.2, Table 5). When humans disagreed with GPT-4 they judged it reasonable in 75% of cases and changed their vote in 34% (§4.2). GPT-4-human agreement climbs from 70% toward nearly 100% as the win-rate gap between compared models grows (§4.2, Figure 2). Position bias persists: swap-consistency is 65% for GPT-4 with the default prompt but only 23.8% for Claude-v1 (Table 2). The authors conclude LLM-as-a-judge approximates controlled and crowdsourced human preferences at over 80% agreement (Abstract).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2: Literature Review > 2.5 Automatic Evaluators and Human Validation

## [52] With Little Power Comes Great Responsibility
- **Bibliography entry:** [52] D. Card, P. Henderson, U. Khandelwal, R. Jia, K. Mahowald, and D. Jurafsky, “With little power comes great responsibility,” in Proc. Conference on Empirical Methods in Natural Language Processing (EMNLP), pp. 9263–9274, 2020.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[55] Card et al. - With little power comes great responsibility.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed, Proceedings of EMNLP 2020, pages 9263–9274 (footer).
- **Problem & context:** Statistical power, the probability of detecting a true effect, is rarely considered in NLP, so many comparisons cannot distinguish real improvements from noise and significant results tend to exaggerate true effects through Type-M and Type-S errors.
- **Approach:** The paper presents a general simulation-based power analysis recipe: define a generative process for assumed effect sizes, simulate datasets, run the planned statistical test, and report the significant proportion (§2, Figure 2). Applications cover classifier comparisons via McNemar's test with agreement and effect-size priors regressed from GLUE and SQuAD 2.0 leaderboard history (§3), machine translation via a randomization test with a Delta-Laplace mixture fitted to FAIRSEQ WMT English-German systems (§4), and a meta-analysis of 117 Likert-style human evaluation comparisons from 41 EMNLP 2019 papers (§5.1).
- **Key findings:** Mean reported improvements sit below the estimated minimum detectable effect for WNLI, MRPC and SST-2, so most future comparisons on those small test sets will be underpowered (§3.3, Table 2). Retrospectively, only 46% of surveyed GLUE state-of-the-art comparisons would have had adequate power and about 51% would have been significant (§3.3). Typical machine translation test sets of 2,000 sentences carry roughly 75% power to detect a 1 BLEU point difference (Abstract, §4, Figure 4). Among human evaluations, 69% used 100 or fewer items and key design details were often unreported (§5.1).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 3: Methodology and Experimental Setup > 3.5 Statistical Analysis

## [53] Inter-Coder Agreement for Computational Linguistics
- **Bibliography entry:** [53] R. Artstein and M. Poesio, “Survey article: Inter-coder agreement for computational linguistics,” Computational Linguistics, vol. 34, no. 4, pp. 555–596, 2008, doi: 10.1162/coli.07-034-R2.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[56] Artstein et al. - Survey article - Inter-coder agreement for computational linguistics.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed journal article, Computational Linguistics, Volume 34, Number 4, 2008 (running header, © 2008 ACL).
- **Problem & context:** Corpus annotation requires demonstrating that coders agree reliably, yet computational linguists adopted kappa-like coefficients without systematically examining their mathematical assumptions or how values should be interpreted.
- **Approach:** This survey derives observed agreement and the shared chance-corrected form (Ao − Ae)/(1 − Ae) used by S (uniform chance assumption), Scott's pi (single pooled distribution) and Cohen's kappa (per-coder marginals), then covers multi-coder generalizations, information-item and unitizing settings, and Krippendorff's alpha including distance-based weighted variants (§2). Worked dialogue-act examples show the same 0.7 observed agreement yielding S of 0.4 but pi of about 0.341 and kappa of about 0.348 under different chance models (Tables 1-2). Sections also treat skewed distributions, significance testing, bootstrapped confidence intervals and stability analysis (§3-4).
- **Key findings:** As a survey it reports no new benchmark numbers; its conclusions are methodological. The choice of chance model can flip reliability verdicts, since pooled versus marginal expected agreement can place a coefficient on different sides of the accepted 0.67 threshold (§1). Interpretation remains unsettled: content-analysis convention treats values above 0.8 as good and 0.67 to 0.8 as tentative, while Krippendorff later calls even 0.8 a low standard and Neuendorf reports 0.9 acceptable to all (§4.1.3). Weighted alpha-like coefficients arguably suit many annotation tasks better than kappa, but make thresholds harder to interpret (Abstract, §4.4).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 3: Methodology and Experimental Setup > 3.5 Statistical Analysis

## [55] Llama Guard: LLM-based Input-Output Safeguard for Human-AI Conversations
- **Bibliography entry:** [55] H. Inan et al., “Llama Guard: LLM-based input-output safeguard for human-AI conversations,” Meta AI, 2023. arXiv:2312.06674 (preprint).
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[58] Inan et al. - Llama Guard - LLM-based input-output safeguard for human-AI conversations.pdf`
- **Publication status:** arXiv preprint (no archival venue shown), consistent with the entry's "(preprint)" grading; the PDF carries only the arXiv stamp (arXiv:2312.06674v1, 7 Dec 2023) and a Meta GenAI date line.
- **Problem & context:** Deployed conversational AI needs guardrails over both user prompts and model outputs, but existing moderation tools enforce fixed policies, do not separate user-side from agent-side risk, and cannot be tailored or self-hosted.
- **Approach:** Llama Guard is Llama2-7b instruction-tuned on 13,997 prompts and responses labeled by Meta's in-house red team under a six-category safety risk taxonomy (violence and hate, sexual content, guns and illegal weapons, regulated substances, suicide and self-harm, criminal planning), split 3:1 for training and evaluation (§2, §3.3, Table 1). One model performs both prompt classification and response classification through changes in instruction wording, emitting safe/unsafe plus violated category codes; new taxonomies are handled by zero-shot or few-shot prompting or further fine-tuning (§3.1-3.2, §4.5). Baselines are the OpenAI Moderation API, Perspective API, Azure Content Safety and GPT-4, scored mainly by AUPRC (§4.3).
- **Key findings:** On its own test set Llama Guard reaches 0.945 AUPRC for prompt and 0.953 for response classification, versus 0.764/0.769 for the OpenAI API and 0.728/0.699 for Perspective (Table 2). On ToxicChat it scores 0.626 against 0.588 and 0.532 (Table 2). On OpenAI's own moderation dataset zero-shot adaptation gives 0.847 versus the API's 0.856, and few-shot prompting lifts it to 0.872 (Table 4, §4.5.1). Fine-tuned on 20% of ToxicChat it already rivals Llama2-7b trained on the full set (§4.5.2, Figure 3).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 3: Methodology and Experimental Setup > 3.5 Statistical Analysis

## [57] NLP Evaluation in Trouble: On the Need to Measure LLM Data Contamination for Each Benchmark
- **Bibliography entry:** [57] O. Sainz, J. Campos, I. García-Ferrero, J. Etxaniz, O. Lopez de Lacalle, and E. Agirre, “NLP evaluation in trouble: On the need to measure LLM data contamination for each benchmark,” in Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EMNLP 2023, pp. 10776–10787, 2023.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[60] Sainz et al. - NLP evaluation in trouble - On the need to measure LLM data contamination for each benchmark.pdf`
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed, Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EMNLP 2023, pages 10776–10787 (footer).
- **Problem & context:** Because LLM pretraining crawls trillions of tokens, researchers often cannot know whether a benchmark was seen during training. Such contamination overestimates a model's performance and can invalidate scientific conclusions drawn with contaminated models.
- **Approach:** This position paper defines three contamination levels, guideline, raw text and annotation contamination, with exposure of the evaluation split treated as most harmful (§3). It maps where contamination enters: pre-training, supervised fine-tuning (including distillation from contaminated teachers), and post-deployment retraining of API products (§4). For measurement it proposes benchmark-overlap percentages via corpus auditing tools for open models, and, for closed models, an extractability-based degree of contamination adapted from Carlini et al.'s memorization definition, computed as the ratio of extractable benchmark examples (§5).
- **Key findings:** As a position paper it reports no experimental metrics; its evidence is documentary. It recounts established contamination incidents: benchmark test splits found in the C4 corpus (Dodge et al.), a GPT-3 filtering bug acknowledged by Brown et al., parts of BIG-bench mixed into an OpenAI training set such that GSM-8K results are not zero-shot, and ChatGPT generating portions of CoNLL2003, casting doubt on published evaluations of it (§2, Appendix A). The call to action asks for automatic and semi-automatic detection measures, a public registry of cases such as the LM Contamination Index, reviewer screening, and flagging of affected published work (§6).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 3: Methodology and Experimental Setup > 3.5 Statistical Analysis
