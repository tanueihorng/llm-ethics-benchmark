- **Bibliography entry:** [8] R. Jin, J. Du, W. Huang, et al., "A comprehensive evaluation of quantization strategies for large language models," in Findings of ACL, 2024 — matches the paper: title L1–L2; authors Renren Jin, Jiangcun Du, Wuwei Huang, Wei Liu, Jian Luan, Bin Wang, Deyi Xiong (L3–L4); venue footer "Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: ACL 2024, pages 12186–12215" (L101–L103). - **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[08] Jin et al. - A comprehensive evaluation of quantization strategies for large language models.pdf` (present in `Sources/`, verified this session via directory listing); full-text extract at `docs/audits/2026-08-25_interim_progress_citation_check/tmp/extract/ref_8.txt` (6,040 lines).
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed archival venue, printed in the PDF itself: Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: ACL 2024, pages 12186–12215, August 11–16, 2024, ©2024 Association for Computational Linguistics (L101–L103). Work performed during the first author's internship at Xiaomi AI Lab (L49); code released at github.com/cordercorder/quant_eval (L325–L326).
- **Problem & context:** Three evaluation gaps motivate the paper: most PTQ studies evaluate pre-trained rather than instruction-tuned LLMs (L20–L21, L289–L293); quantized-model evaluation is usually limited to language modeling plus a few classification tasks (L23–L24, L294–L298); and the correlation between quantized LLMs' perplexity and their downstream benchmark performance is poorly understood (L21–L23, L298–L304).
- **Approach:** A structured framework over three dimensions — knowledge & capacity, alignment, efficiency (L25–L27, L398–L403) — applied to ten benchmarks (Table 1, L105–L267): MMLU (14,042 test samples, L127), C-EVAL (12,342), FLORES-200 En↔Zh translation BLEU (1,012, L151), CNN/DailyMail and XSum summarisation ROUGE (11,490/L169, 11,334/L179), GSM8K (1,319, L189), SNLI (10,000, L199), and — for alignment, mapped onto Askell et al.'s helpful/honest/harmless criteria (L904–L909) — FollowBench instruction-following HSR/SSR/CSL (820, L209; GPT-4-as-judge for SSR, L253), TruthfulQA MC1 (817, L220), BBQ social bias (58,492, L230). Models are the instruction-tuned Qwen-Chat family at 7B/14B/72B (L926–L936); methods are post-training quantization only: LLM.int8() (8-bit only), GPTQ and SpQR ("8, 4, 3, and 2" bits, L1262–L1264), calibrated on 128 random examples from Alpaca-style instruction data (Taori et al. 2023; Peng et al. 2023, L1265–L1267); SpQR runs via fake quantization since no CUDA kernel existed (L2913–L2915). Efficiency is measured as memory (GB) and decoding speed (tokens/s) at 256-token input/512-token output on A100 80GB SXM GPUs (L900–L902, L2138). Scope nuance: all three evaluated methods quantize weights while activations stay high precision (L2287–L2291); weight-activation schemes such as SmoothQuant appear only in Related Work (L349–L352), so the study compares weight-side PTQ strategies across bit widths, not weight-only vs weight-activation pipelines.
- **Key findings:** (1) Capability: 8-bit is near-lossless; 4-bit keeps performance comparable to non-quantized counterparts on most benchmarks; degradation starts at 3 bits or lower, and 2-bit GPTQ collapses into incoherent, instruction-blind text (L1700–L1716; e.g., Qwen-7B MMLU average 55.80 → 23.74⋆ GPTQ vs 52.49⋆ SpQR at 2-bit, L4236/L4321–L4322). (2) Perplexity is a reliable proxy: average absolute Pearson correlation with benchmark performance 0.7895 over 4-/3-bit GPTQ+SpQR models (Table 3, L1859–L2032; L2023–L2027); 2-bit GPTQ perplexity exceeds 38,000 (L1726–L1727), e.g. WikiText2 123030.34 for Qwen-7B (Table 7, L3859). (3) SpQR's 2-bit survival depends on isolating unstructured outlier weights kept at 16-bit: dropping isolation inflates perplexity even at group size 16, whereas enlarging group size "from 16 to 128" costs little (controlled experiments, L2046–L2060, Table 4 L2139–L2210); high-precision outliers grow from 0.003% (8-bit) to 11.336% (2-bit) for Qwen-7B (Table 5, L2234–L2237). (4) Efficiency: GPTQ/LLM.int8() cut memory (Qwen-72B: 138.44 GB → 77.85 GB at 8-bit GPTQ, L758/L793) but generally do not speed up decoding because activations remain high precision and hardware lacks mixed-precision acceleration — only the 72B model shows significant speed-up, at 4-bit (L2279–L2295); SpQR's studied implementation saves no memory at all (fake quantization, L2265–L2271); baseline Qwen-7B runs 15.14 GB / 37.67 tokens-per-s (L468/L470). (5) At similar memory, prefer a larger quantized model: Qwen-14B 8-bit GPTQ (17.92 GB; corrected 2026-08-25 — the number audit caught the original text attaching LLM.int8()'s 15.91 GB cell to GPTQ; INT-8 row reads LLM.int8 15.91 / GPTQ 17.92, Table 8) or 4-bit GPTQ (12.03 GB) costs about the same as Qwen-7B BFloat16 (15.14 GB) yet outperforms it on most benchmarks (L2300–L2307; Table 8 L4086–L4117). On ability vs safety: capability is preserved down to 4 bits for all three methods, with SpQR clearly best at extreme (≤3-bit, especially 2-bit) compression; alignment signals are mixed — social bias shows no consistent direction under quantization (can exacerbate or alleviate, L2365–L2367), truthfulness can fall significantly (Qwen-7B TruthfulQA MC1 0.38 → 0.30⋆ at 2-bit GPTQ, Table 13 L5404–L5467; also flagged in Ethical Considerations L2368–L2372), and instruction-following degrades at low bits (Qwen-7B HSR 0.40 → 0.00 at 2-bit GPTQ, Table 14 L5636–L5704). The paper measures no harmfulness/refusal-compliance metric anywhere (no HarmBench-style test in the extract), so it makes no claim about attack success rate.
- **Cited in the report at:** Verified against the live builder this session: the Jin et al. entry sits at position 10 of the references array (builder L1078) and is bracket-cited exactly once — Chapter 2 > §2.4 "Quantization and Behavioural Effects" (builder L446): "A comprehensive multi-benchmark evaluation of quantization strategies finds that headline benchmark scores are largely retained at four bits, while cautioning that evaluations limited to language modelling and a few classification tasks can miss shifts on other behavioural dimensions such as alignment [10]." There is no Chapter 1 citation of this paper in the current builder, and bracket [8] currently denotes QLoRA (builder L445 body text; refs L1076). The previously recorded locations ("Chapter 1 > 1.1 Background and Motivation + Chapter 2 > 2.2 Quantization Methods and Capability Effects") do not match the current build — see Discrepancy check.
- **Limitations & caveats:** The authors flag possible pre-training/test contamination that could inflate scores and limit generalization to other benchmarks (L2337–L2344); experiments cover only the Qwen-Chat family, so findings may not transfer to other LLMs given differences in data, hyperparameters, and architectures (L2345–L2354). Additional caveats material to reuse: SpQR was run fake-quantized, so its measured memory/speed reflects the implementation, not the method's potential (L2265–L2271, L2909–L2916); the perplexity–performance link is correlational, computed on averaged scores of 4-/3-bit models (Table 3 caption L2029–L2032); all benchmarks are rule-based except FollowBench's GPT-4-judged SSR component (Table 1, L248–L253); efficiency uses one GPU type (A100 80GB SXM) and one fixed token profile (256-in/512-out, L900–L902, L2138); and the evaluated methods do not include NF4/QLoRA quantization itself (NF4 appears only as related work, L354–L355), nor any harmfulness/safety-refusal benchmark.
- **Relevance to this FYP:** Indirect but supportive. The paper's central result — 4-bit weight-side PTQ broadly preserves benchmark ability while ≤3-bit is where behaviour breaks (L1700–L1716, Conclusion L2324–L2326) — is consistent with this FYP's operating range (NF4 4-bit main study plus an INT8 point) and with its finding that quantization costs show up on the capability side rather than as safety erosion; its perplexity-as-proxy result (avg |Pearson| 0.7895, L2026) cautions that capability anchors must be watched even when headline scores hold. It cannot corroborate the FYP's safety-null directly: the models are far larger (7B–72B chat models vs this project's ≈1.7–7.2B pairs), the methods differ (GPTQ/SpQR/LLM.int8(), not BitsAndBytes NF4), and no harmfulness or over-refusal metric is evaluated — its "alignment" axis (instruction following, truthfulness, social bias) is complementary evidence only. Its engineering conclusion that quantized inference often slows down without kernel/hardware support (L2279–L2299) matches this project's on-the-fly BitsAndBytes loading context, where memory savings, not speed, are the expected benefit. Citation-fitness note: the current v5 report's single use of this source (§2.4, "headline benchmark scores are largely retained at four bits", builder L446) is supported by the paper (Abstract L29–L31; L1700–L1716; L2324–L2326).
