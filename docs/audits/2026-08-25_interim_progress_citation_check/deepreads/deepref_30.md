## Upgraded summary

- **Bibliography entry:** [30] K. Chen et al., "Assessing safety risks and quantization-aware safety patching for quantized large language models," 2025. Venue now verified against the PDF itself (see next bullet); the entry carries no venue and should be graded as a peer-reviewed conference paper once updated.
- **Publication status:** Peer-reviewed — the PDF's own copyright block reads "Proceedings of the 42nd International Conference on Machine Learning, Vancouver, Canada. PMLR 267, 2025" (extract lines 71–73), i.e. ICML 2025, PMLR volume 267. Verified this session from the primary artifact. Page range and any arXiv ID are not present in the extract and were not verified.
- **Problem & context:** Prior work suggested calibration-dataset-free quantization can compromise LLM safety (lines 27–31, 50–56, 151–162), motivating two questions: how much do different quantization techniques and calibration datasets degrade safety, and how can the decline be mitigated while keeping utility (81–84)?
- **Approach — assessment:** Covers four methods spanning PTQ-without-fine-tuning (AWQ), PTQ-with-fine-tuning (AQLM), QAT full-parameter (LLM-QAT) and QAT parameter-efficient (QLoRA) at INT4 and INT8 (273–283; Table 1 at 191–213), on Llama-2-7B-Chat and Gemma-7B-Instruct (249–250). Three ten-sample quantization-assisting datasets of graded risk: directly harmful (Risk-III, AdvBench instructions + harmful responses), indirectly harmful (Risk-II, non-toxic instructions paired with compliance-inducing responses), benign (Risk-I, UltraChat) (238–247). Safety = Attack Success Rate on AdvBench-style harmful instructions for the dataset-assisted methods; AWQ (no assisting dataset) is evaluated under decoding attacks that search sampling parameters (temperature/top-k/top-p) with GPT-4 selecting the best of 49 responses per request (286–292, 749–753, 1662–1673). Utility = MT-Bench and AlpacaEval (264–271). An appendix re-scores with the official HarmBench binary classifier plus a GPT-4 1–5 harmfulness score "to reduce the risk of false positives" (1678–1682; Table 9, 1719–1743).
- **Approach — patching:** Proposes **Q-resafe** (Quantization-aware safety patching; named at 34–36 and 88–89, expanded at 95–96), a three-step post-quantization repair: (1) build a safety-patching preference set by prompting both the pre-quantization and quantized models on calibration prompts and labelling pre-quant answers as winners (638–704); (2) optimize a DPO loss over a LoRA adapter constrained to Q = Q′ + Quant(M_Q ⊙ AB), i.e. updates recompressed into the same low precision (569–602); (3) periodically identify safety-critical weights via SNIP-style magnitude-of-gradient scores and update only the top-τ percentile (653–688; Algorithm 1, 607–636). Defaults: τ=0.6, LoRA rank r=2048, re-evaluation interval K=1000 (735–737). If the pre-quantization model is unavailable, other well-aligned LLMs can substitute (643–645).
- **Key findings (exact anchors):** All four techniques degrade safety, worse at lower bit-widths and with harmful calibration data (130–140, 493–537, 562–567). Headlines: under direct-harm patching conditions, baseline quantizations raise ASR by up to 92.3% (Llama) and 66.7% (Gemma) over pre-quantization while Q-resafe adds only 13.6% and 1.8% (745–747); benign setting: +16.6%/+11.5% vs +1.5%/+0.9%, reached in one epoch (762–771); indirect-harm: +82.6%/+59.2% vs +13.3%/+5.5% (783–791); fine-tuning-free AWQ comparison: AWQ adds up to +7.3%/+5.8% vs Q-resafe +0.8%/+0.4% (749–759). Ablations: dropping safety-critical identification (τ=0) explodes ASR from 1.6% to 42.2% (1086–1089; Table 5, 1014–1048); Q-resafe beats SFT/DPO at lower GPU-hours (Table 6, e.g. QLoRA+SFT 26.9% ASR @3.4 h vs Q-resafe 2.4% @1.2 h, 1063–1079, 1138–1148); ASR grows monotonically 8→4→3→2-bit with the steepest jump between INT4 and 3-bit (1150–1164; Table 7, 1098–1136); appendix bnb check: LLM.int8() 19.2→5.2, NF4 23.9→5.5, FP4 35.2→6.0 ASR after patching (1749–1751, Table 10 at 1763–1777). HarmBench-classifier re-check tracks suffix-match ASR within ≈1 point on Llama INT4/INT8 (Table 9, 1729–1743).

- **Limitations & caveats:**
  - Only two ~7B chat models (Llama-2-7B-Chat, Gemma-7B-Instruct; 249–250); no smaller-model evidence, so transfer to ≤4B models (this FYP's range) is untested here.
  - Safety rests on one harm family: AdvBench-style ASR under specific system/AOA prompts (Table 8, 1686–1717); the AWQ arm's numbers come from decoding attacks (best-of-49 sampling search judged by GPT-4, 1662–1673) — an adversarial-decoding threat model, not ordinary greedy use.
  - Internal prose-vs-table inconsistencies: §3.2 text gives Llama/AQLM indirect-harm ASR as 73.5% (480) while Table 3's INT4 block shows 75.5 (334); Gemma/AQLM direct-harm 67.3% in text (484) vs 55.4 in the table's INT4 block (371); LLM-QAT text "82.1% and … 83.7%" (459–462) vs table 82.9 and 71.2 (Llama INT4 indirect/direct, 336–341). Anyone quoting this paper should prefer table cells and say which block was used.
  - Unresolved internal contradiction on adapter size: main text sets LoRA rank r=2048 (736) while Appendix A.1 lists LoRA r=128, α=256 (1610–1611).
  - No seeds/variance/confidence intervals reported anywhere in the extract; single-run point estimates.
  - Headline §5.1 deltas are prose summaries of Figure 1 plots whose underlying values are not recoverable from the extract; appendix Tables 11–13 corroborate magnitudes but use different training schedules.
  - The indirectly-harmful (AOA) dataset is intentionally not released (1631–1632), limiting reproduction of that arm; the reproducibility promise (1204–1208) cannot be checked from the extract.
  - Utility preservation is relative to strong baselines: Table 5 shows MT-Bench sliding 7.3→6.4 as τ goes 1.0→0 (1043–1048), i.e. the selective-weight trick trades some utility for safety.

- **Relevance to this FYP:**
  - Independent peer-reviewed support for the premise that quantization is not safety-neutral, and that lower precision hurts more (562–567) — context for our NF4-primary/INT8-secondary design and for interpreting small, sign-varying ASR deltas in our five matched pairs.
  - Their appendix C.1 evaluates exactly our BitsAndBytes family — LLM.int8(), NF4, FP4 — finding pre-patch ASR 19.2–35.2 restored to 5.2–6.0 by Q-resafe (1746–1777). Their setting (Llama-2-7B, AdvBench prompts, their decoding protocol) differs from ours, so this corroborates plausibility of quantization-induced safety movement rather than contradicting our 512-budget null result; both can be true given different models, prompts and scorers.
  - Methodological parallel: they adopt the official HarmBench classifier to reduce false positives (1678–1679), the same primary-scorer decision as our D16; their Table 9 suffix-vs-classifier agreement within ~1 point (1729–1743) is a small external sanity point for judge-based ASR.
  - Contrast that sharpens our contribution: they repair quantized models with a trained patch (DPO on selected weights) and require the pre-quantization reference model; we measure what plain on-the-fly quantization does to aligned behaviour without any repair, jointly with capability anchors (MMLU/ARC) — their paper measures capability only as MT-Bench/AlpacaEval helpfulness, never jointly against a fixed capability benchmark per pair.
  - Report usage check: the report cites this work only as background ("confirms quantization-induced safety degradation and proposes a quantization-aware patching method to repair it", builder line 446) — that sentence is entailed by the abstract (31–37) and Sections 3–5. One cosmetic defect for the citation checker: the report's bibliography entry prefixes the title with "Q-resafe:" (builder line 1083), which does not match the PDF's actual title (extract lines 1–2).
