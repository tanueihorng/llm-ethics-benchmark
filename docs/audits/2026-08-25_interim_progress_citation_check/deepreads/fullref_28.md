- **Problem & context:** Quantization research is overwhelmingly evaluated in English only, and no prior work had characterised quantization's effect on multilingual generation, although underserved languages and severe compute constraints geographically co-occur (the "low-resource double bind"), making cheap-and-reliable multilingual service a prerequisite for wide adoption (§1, lines 46–57). Prior compression work suggests long-tail features are disparately treated, motivating concern for under-represented languages (lines 59–63).
- **Approach:** Four SOTA multilingual LLMs across three sizes 8B–103B, up to 23 languages (lines 80–82): Command R+ (103B), Command R (35B), and Aya 23 (35B, 8B) (§3, lines 245–248). Quantizers (lines 250–268): for Command R/R+, weight-only W8 (per-column scaling), W4-g (group-wise GPTQ), weight-and-activation W8A8 (per-column weights, per-token activations), plus 103B-only W8A8-SmoothQuant and a naive column-wise W4; SmoothQuant/GPTQ calibrated on 128 English samples (lines 260–262). For Aya 23, bitsandbytes: LLM.int8() 8-bit and NF4 4-bit (lines 263–268). Languages: 10 primary (ar, fr, de, en, es, it, pt, ko, ja, zh; lines 273–274), extended to 23 via the Aya evaluation suite (+cs, el, he, hi, id, nl, fa, pl, ro, ru, tr, uk, vi; lines 331–334). Evaluation triangulates automatic suites (mMMLU 5-shot Google-translated MMLU; MGSM math in 5 languages; FLORES-200 En↔L2 SacreBLEU; fastText-based monolingual/cross-lingual Language Confusion line-level pass rate; unseen discriminative XWinograd/XCOPA/XStoryCloze + Okapi mMMLU/MGSM/Belebele; §3.1, lines 300–334), GPT-4-turbo LLM-as-a-Judge and a multilingual RM-as-a-Judge over the same prompts as the human study (§3.3, lines 690–701), and human pairwise preference evaluation in es/fr/ja/ko/en on 150 complex Internal-suite prompts and 150 Aya Dolly-200 prompts per language (§3.2, lines 351–369). Primary statistic is relative %∆ vs the FP16 model (Eq. 5, lines 276–280), averaged over 5 runs (line 284–285).
- **Key findings:** (1) Automatic metrics severely underestimate damage: for the 103B W4-g Command model of Figure 1 (caption, lines 34–37), automatic averages fall only −0.3% (French) and −1.7% (Japanese) while human evaluators report −16.6% and −16.0% (Finding 1, lines 89–93; Table 8 anchors: French Internal W4-g −16.6% lines 1684–1686, Japanese Internal W4-g −16.0% lines 1692–1694). (2) Languages are disparately affected, degradation negatively correlated with training-set size (R²=0.24 at W8 → 0.63 at W4 against mC4 data volume, line 1250), non-Latin-script worst: across tasks at 103B, Latin-script −0.7% vs non-Latin −1.9% (Finding 2, lines 97–101; Table 3 103B W4 row, lines 833–843); for the 8B Aya, −3.0% vs −3.7% (line 101). (3) Challenging tasks degrade fastest: mathematical reasoning −13.1% (35B W4-g MGSM; worked example in Table 1 caption, lines 561–566; as bad as −17.3% in Chinese, line 1029), human-judged realistic prompts −10.5% (Internal avg at W4-g, Table 8 line 1706), LLM-as-a-Judge −25.9% (Internal avg, W4 column, Table 7 line 1514). (4) Occasionally quantization helps: e.g. +1.3% average across tasks for the 35B at W8A8 (Finding 4, lines 106–110; Table 3 35B W8A8 row +1.3% in all three summary columns, lines 885–897), echoing Badshah & Sajjad (2024) on English. English-vs-non-English: under most settings English degrades less than the average of all other languages, with the largest gap on 35B MGSM where W8A8/W4-g hurt far more outside English (en −2.2% vs All −13.1% at W4-g; Table 5, lines 1214–1224, prose 1252–1254); Aya is likewise worse on average off-English at W4 (lines 1254–1257). Scale interaction: at the most extreme quantization smaller models are more sensitive — Command W4-g: 103B −0.9% vs 35B −2.8% overall and −2.9% vs −13.1% on MGSM; Aya 35B vs 8B: −2.9% vs −3.7% overall, largest gap on Belebele (−5.9% vs −8.5%) (§4.4, lines 1262–1269).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2 > 2.2 Quantization Methods and Capability Effects (confirmed: the interim progress report's chapter source cites "Marchisio et al." with bracket [13] inside the §2.2 content block — heading added at `scripts/build_rewritten_fyp_report_v1.py` line 987, citing paragraph at lines 1024–1031, before the §2.3 heading at line 1040; TOC entry "2.2 Quantization Methods and Capability Effects" at line 672 and in `scripts/build_rewritten_fyp_interim_progress.py` line 35. Secondary observations: the v5 canonical `make report` build contains no citation of this paper (grep count 0), and the v6 submission candidate cites it at Chapter 2 > 2.4 "Quantization and Behavioural Effects" (`build_fyp_report_v6.js` lines 469, 474) plus a comparison-table row in §2.5 (line 488).)
- **Limitations & caveats:** Authors' own (§7, lines 1856–1873): only two model families (Command R/R+, Aya 23) were tested — trends are expected but not guaranteed to generalize, and differently optimized or task-specialized (code/math) models may behave differently; only model-supported languages were evaluated, and deterioration is likely even larger for absent or severely under-represented languages, where benchmark and annotator availability also limits evaluation. Additional caveats grounded in the paper: training mixtures are not released, so the data-size correlation is an inference from an mC4 proxy rather than measured training data (lines 1241–1244); the judge and human evaluations cover the 103B Command model only (Tables 7–8 captions, lines 1618, 1756) in five languages; the LLM and RM judges disagree on the direction of some Dolly-set effects, which the authors flag as needing further study (lines 1655–1659); Figure 1's automatic-vs-human contrast excludes MGSM because it is unavailable for Korean (footnote 1, lines 116–118); the paper studies capability and generation quality, not safety behaviour — no safety benchmark appears anywhere in its task set (§3.1–3.3).
- **Relevance to this FYP:** This FYP's main precision point (on-the-fly bitsandbytes NF4 4-bit, plus an INT8/LLM.int8 point) is exactly the quantizer family the paper applies to Aya 23 (NF4 datatype + LLM.int8(); lines 263–268), so it is a direct methodological precedent for our BitsAndBytesConfig load-time approach. Its measurement lesson motivates this project's scorer-validation layer: if automatic metrics can understate quantization damage by roughly an order of magnitude (−1.7% vs −16.0% Japanese, lines 22–24, 89–93), then the HarmBench/XSTest scorers themselves must be validated rather than assumed — the same argument the interim report makes when citing this work in §2.2. Its scale finding cuts toward caution for us: the smallest model studied is 8B and smaller models degrade more (lines 1262–1269), while this FYP evaluates 1.7–7.2B models — i.e., entirely below the paper's range — so capability anchors (MMLU, ARC-Challenge) alongside safety metrics are necessary, and effect sizes may exceed those reported here. Its English-vs-non-English result (English degrades least; lines 1252–1254) bounds what our English-only HarmBench/XSTest/MMLU/ARC evidence can claim: null capability deltas on English benchmarks do not rule out larger multilingual degradation in the same quantized checkpoints — honest Chapter 8/9 limitation material. Conversely, the paper offers no evidence about ASR or over-refusal, so it cannot support any direct safety-behaviour claim in this report.
