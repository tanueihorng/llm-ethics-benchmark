## Upgraded summary

- **Bibliography entry:** [21] S. Badshah and H. Sajjad, "Quantifying the capabilities of LLMs across scale and precision," 2024. arXiv:2405.03146 (preprint). Title and both authors verified against the PDF title block (extract 1–5); the extract carries the stamp `arXiv:2405.03146v2 [cs.LG] 8 May 2024` (line 9), so the repo's ID and year are right and the specific artifact read is **v2**.
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[29] Badshah et al. - Quantifying the capabilities of LLMs across scale and precision.pdf` — the filename prefix `[29]` is **not** an error but a second numbering system: the citing interim report (`docs/rewritten_fyp_interim_v3.docx`) prints this reference as **[29]** in its own reference list (verified by extracting the docx text this session), while this citation-check audit renumbers it [21]; the PDF was renamed to the interim report's numbering (per PROJECT_LOG 2026-08-22 row). Extracted text identity: title/authors at 1–5 match, arXiv v2 stamp at 9.
- **Publication status:** **arXiv preprint (v2, 8 May 2024), no archival venue shown** — graded honestly from the PDF itself: the only provenance marker anywhere in the 2067-line extract is the arXiv stamp (line 9), and an absence grep for accepted/camera-ready/copyright/published-in strings returns nothing. The paper uses *ACL-template furniture (a numbered "Limitations" section at 663–690 and an Ethics Statement at 692–700), consistent with an ACL-format submission, but the artifact contains no acceptance or venue evidence, so it must be cited as a preprint.
- **Problem & context:** Scale is widely credited for LLM gains, but large models are expensive to run in resource-constrained settings, so practitioners choose between smaller models (e.g., Llama 7B vs 70B) and quantized larger ones — and the authors state the combined effect of scale and quantization on downstream performance "needs thorough examination" (abstract 20–26). They motivate via training/inference costs (GPT-3 ≈1300 MWh training electricity, ≈288 single-V100 training years, five A100-80GB GPUs for fp16 inference; 74–82) and note prior work suggests 4-bit offers optimal scaling benefits while task-level effects remain unclear (101–103).
- **Approach:** Zero-shot evaluation (explicitly no examples and no chain-of-thought, 204–205) of instruct models from two families: Llama 2-Chat — 13B exists in the family but only **7B and 70B are evaluated** (220–225) — plus Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 and Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1, referred to as Mistral 7B / Mixtral 8×7B (233–237). Four precision points per model: FP32 and FP16 baselines (FP16 designated the comparison baseline after observing FP16≈FP32, 275–277), 8-bit via LLM.int8() and 4-bit via QLoRA's NF4, both implemented with the bitsandbytes library (242–258); the half/full-precision baselines run through PyTorch's native float16/float32 dtypes rather than bitsandbytes (259–264). Decoding: temperature 0.6, repetition penalty 1.2, top-k 50, top-p 0.9; batch 8 for 7B models, 2 otherwise (265–269). Prompts combine role-playing, templated, and direct-to-detail strategies (210–215; examples in Table 4, 1337–1380). Tasks span NLU (summarization CNN/Daily Mail + SAMSum scored ROUGE-1; translation FLoRes-200 over 9 HRLs + 3 LRLs scored ChrF++; NusaX sentiment scored Macro-F1), ten reasoning categories (deductive, inductive, abductive, temporal, spatial, mathematical, commonsense, causal, multi-hop, analogical), hallucination (TruthfulQA) and COVID-19 misinformation detection (Table 2, 279–345; Appendix A, 1053–1318), with 30–660 sampled test items per task (190–192) due to compute limits; standard NLU tasks are scored automatically while reasoning/hallucination/misinformation use human evaluation (193–197).
- **Key findings:** (1) **Scale helps in most tasks**: larger variants generally beat smaller ones within a family at similar precision (abstract 29–31; §1 116–118; conclusion 646–648), e.g., StepGame basic-cardinal and EntailmentBank (362–364). (2) **Scale benefits are task-dependent**: even the largest models fail Letter String Analogies (368–370); the conclusion names analogical, deductive, and certain spatial reasoning as marginal-or-no-gain cases when scaling up (649–652); in SpartQA (hard), Mixtral 8×7B comes in slightly below Mistral 7B (§1 156–158) — cross-checking the appendix tables shows this holds at FP16/FP32 (53.125 vs 50, extract 1666/1713) but **reverses** at 4-bit (34.375 vs 43.75, 1448/1495) and 8-bit (my reconstruction from Tables 5–6, flagged as my inference, not stated in the paper). (3) **Larger models are more truthful**, deviating from Inverse Scaling Law expectations (489–494); on TruthfulQA the Llama family shows marked precision sensitivity — the 70B jumps from 43.94% at 8-bit to 54.55% at FP16 (claim 497–503; Table 7 cells 43.94/54.55 at 1893/1899) — while Mistral 7B is flat at 77.27 across all four precisions (1851–1863). On COVID fact-checking, larger models win the scientific subset (452–457) but smaller models detect social myths better, with **Mistral 7B outperforming all other models — social context depends less on scale** (§1 158; §3.1 473–478). (4) **Precision resilience concentrates in larger models**: they maintain high accuracy even at 4-bit on numerous tasks (abstract 31–36; conclusion 653–655); Llama 2-Chat is less impacted by 4-/8-bit than the Mistral family on reasoning average (417–420); mathematical reasoning is relatively unaffected by precision at all sizes (427–429); StepGame even improves slightly at 4-bit vs FP16 for Llama 2-Chat 7B (439–441); the paper also reports the smaller-model counter-side, e.g., Llama 2-Chat 7B scoring 88 at 4-bit vs 84 at FP16 on the scientific subset (458–460; Table 7 cells at 1870/1882). (5) **Deployment recommendation**: within a fixed memory budget, a larger 4-bit model often beats a smaller higher-precision one — a 70B at 4-bit needs ~42 GB (Table 1, line 148; §1 167–171) and "consistently delivers superior performance" across tasks (164–171). (6) NLU detail: ROUGE-1 is higher on SAMSum than CNN/Daily Mail and the Llama-70B stays robust across precisions (528–544); MT favors the Mistral family with gains of larger models more pronounced English↔low-resource languages (553–566); on NusaX sentiment Llama-70B is the worst on English (571–573), all models struggle on Buginese, and 4-/8-bit differences vs FP16 are minimal (574–578).
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2 > 2.2 — verified directly against the primary citing artifact this session: `docs/rewritten_fyp_interim_v3.docx`, §2.2 "Quantization Methods and Capability Effects", first paragraph cites it once in the body (as that document's ref **[29]**): "Scaling work shows that larger models often tolerate reduced precision better than smaller ones, but the relationship depends on model scale and task [29]." That sentence is supported by the source (tolerance: abstract 31–36, §1 159–163, conclusion 653–655; task dependence: 116–158, 364–387, 649–652).
- **Limitations & caveats:** The authors' own §6 (667–690): limited sample sets threaten external validity (full datasets would strengthen results); zero-shot dependency may understate potential performance since more shots can help (citing Brown et al., 2020); prompt wording can influence results; ROUGE-1/ChrF++/F1 may not capture understanding richness; and — notably candid — the observed precision-resilience **cannot distinguish inherent model ability from the effectiveness of the particular quantization techniques used** (685–690). Additional caveats from this read: per-cell samples are tiny (30 items ⇒ 1 item ≈ 3.33 points; TruthfulQA n=66 at 1307), yet no statistical test, confidence interval, or variance estimate appears anywhere in the extract (absence grep: t-test/p-value/confidence interval/bootstrap/standard deviation → no match), so cross-precision differences within a few points are uninterpretable as effects; human-evaluation protocol details (annotators, blinding, agreement) are not reported; the 42 GB figure is an *estimate* table credited to Kaplan/Hoffmann-style arithmetic (caption 150–152), not a measured footprint; and all conclusions bind to LLM.int8()/QLoRA-NF4 via bitsandbytes on Llama-2/Mistral chat models — the paper's own confound admission limits transfer to other quantizers.
- **Relevance to this FYP:** This paper is the closest published antecedent of the FYP's core interpretation frame: capability degradation is the expected, directionally-consistent effect of going to 4-bit while its size varies by family and task (417–420, 649–655), which independently supports the FYP's matched-pair, four-family design and its practice of reading safety deltas against capability anchors rather than assuming uniform quantization effects. Its method choice — NF4 weights-only via bitsandbytes applied at load time (247–258) — is the same mechanism as the FYP's on-the-fly NF4 loading, making its qualitative directions more transferable than GPTQ-based studies. Its FP16≈FP32 equivalence observation (275–277) parallels the FYP's fp16-baseline convention. Boundaries to keep honest when citing: the paper studies 7B–70B chat models where the headline recommendation ("deploy the bigger model at 4-bit", 164–171) presupposes a larger sibling existing within budget — in the FYP's compact 1.7–7.2B regime that recommendation does not apply, and the paper itself shows scale gains are task-dependent anyway; its battery contains no safety/refusal benchmarks, so it evidences the capability side of the trade-off only and says nothing about harmful compliance or over-refusal; and given its absent significance testing (see caveats), it should be cited for directional findings, never for effect magnitudes.
