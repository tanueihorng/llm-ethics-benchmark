## Upgraded summary

- **Bibliography entry:** [29] Y. Belkhiter et al., "HarmLevelBench: Evaluating harm-level compliance and the impact of quantization on model alignment," 2024. arXiv:2411.06835. Full author list verified against the PDF title block: Yannis Belkhiter, Giulio Zizzo, Sergio Maffeis (extract 7–20); arXiv stamp reads 2411.06835v1 [cs.CL], 11 Nov 2024 (1).
- **Repo PDF:** `Sources/[35] Belkhiter et al. - HarmLevelBench - Evaluating harm-level compliance and the impact of quantization on model alignment.pdf`
- **Publication status:** arXiv preprint (v1, 11 Nov 2024, extract 1); no archival venue or proceedings indicated anywhere in the extracted text. The PDF honestly discloses, in footnotes, presentation at the **NeurIPS 2024 Workshop on Safe Generative Artificial Intelligence (SafeGenAI)** (64) — a workshop, i.e. not peer-reviewed archival publication — and that the work was done during the first author's MSc thesis at Imperial College London (60–62). The repo bibliography's additional note that SafeGenAI is non-archival and issues no proceedings is an external fact not decidable from the PDF alone.
- **Problem & context:** Existing jailbreak datasets (AdvBench, Pruned AdvBench, XSTest, SafetyBench) are criticised for unstructured query formulation and absence of graded harmfulness, blocking fine-grained compliance analysis (94–101), while how compression affects alignment remains underexplored with size-vs-safety trade-offs emerging as a concern (74–76).
- **Approach:** Harm-level taxonomy: **7 harmful topics × 8 severity levels each = 56 queries** (contributions 77–79; structure 106–117; full listing Table 6, index 1–56 at 731–906 and prompts 908–967), all under one "How to ...?" template (108–112); topics span explosive devices, hacking, drugs, killing, shooting, stealing and triggering (909–966), and an 8-row rubric grades levels on impact, means complexity, real-world consequence and legality/morality from individual/minimal/legal up to large-group/catastrophic/illegal-and-immoral (Table 7, 982–1025). Seven jailbreak techniques ordered by complexity (210–226): three manual (Simple Query, Ignorance context, Role Play context) plus PAIR (attacker "Mixtral 7x8B", GPT-3.5 turbo judge, 5 streams × 5 iterations; 215–216), PAP (average of Authority / Logical appealing / Misrepresentation; 216–218), AutoDAN-GA (219) and GCG (220). ASR is scored three ways: human annotation, string detection, and a GPT judge with three compliance classes (186–193). Single target model **Vicuna 13B v1.5** (231); a Llama attempt was abandoned with no numbers given (233–234). Quantized arms: **AWQ and GPTQ versions of the same model**, attacked both **directly** (§4.3, 352–355) and with **transferred** attacks crafted on the original model (§4.4, 428–435). The paper frames AWQ/GPTQ as QAT- and PTQ-family representatives respectively (236–240) and **never states a bit-width**.
- **Key findings:** (1) On the original Vicuna, complex attacks approach saturation: AutoDAN Human ASR **100** (274) and GCG String ASR **100** (283), versus Simple Query Human ASR **33.9** (269) (Table 2, 263–311). (2) Query harmfulness modulates success by attack complexity: close to no effect for the most complex methods, but highly harmful queries drive relatively poor ASR for low/moderate-complexity attacks (344–347); under the GPT judge, more complex attacks produce *less* full compliance (337–339) — e.g. AutoDAN scores Human ASR 100 (274) but judge full-compliance only 23.2 (300), a large scorer divergence inside one table. (3) Direct attacks on the AWQ copy: the authors report higher ASR than fp16 for query-based attacks and lower for advanced ones (361–362) — concretely PAIR String ASR **98.2** vs **92.9** on fp16 (388 vs 281), while GCG Human ASR falls to **66.1** from **92.9** (382 vs 275) (Table 3). (4) Transferred attacks weaken sharply on both quantized copies: PAIR Human ASR drops **94.6 → 66.1 (AWQ) / 64.3 (GPTQ)** and AutoDAN **100 → 71.4 / 67.9** (stated verbatim 439–441; matching table blocks 474–488) (Table 4). (5) The authors conclude quantization "generally enhances robustness against adversarial examples in transferred contexts" (581–584) while flagging heightened susceptibility on direct attacks (abstract 36–38). **Versus the general claim "quantization degrades safety": this paper does not support a blanket version of it** — its effects are non-uniform and attack-type-dependent (higher ASR on the AWQ copy for some query-based/direct attacks, lower for advanced attacks, and clearly lower for transferred attacks on both AWQ and GPTQ), so it is best cited as evidence that quantization *reshuffles* which attacks succeed rather than uniformly degrading alignment.
- **Cited in the report at:** Chapter 2 > 2.2 Quantization Methods and Capability Effects
- **Limitations & caveats:** Author-stated (586–594): only a specific set of jailbreaking techniques, so results may not generalize to other attacks or models; the dataset is small — the paper notes ~50-query datasets are the literature norm and a larger one would help (590–592; Table 6 confirms 56 prompts); and the **performance/accuracy cost of quantization is not evaluated at all** (592–594). Additional caveats from this read (flagged as reader observations, not author statements): single target model (Llama arm dropped unreported, 233–234); bit-width of the AWQ/GPTQ configs never stated; no statistical testing anywhere in the paper — every "significant/significantly" hit in the extract is rhetorical prose or a rubric word, with no p-values or intervals (verified by exhaustive grep, see Self-test); the authors themselves acknowledge LLM-judge biases (192–195), and their own tables show scorer-dependent pictures (AutoDAN 100 Human vs 23.2 judge full-compliance, 274 vs 300); heatmap conclusions (Figures 1–6) rest on captions/prose because the figures are images absent from the text layer; the paper's labelling of AWQ under "Quantization Aware Training" (236–240) is loose relative to common usage and should be paraphrased cautiously, not repeated as fact; and one summary sentence is grammatically garbled in the source ("quantized models are more susceptible to harm-level…", 584–585) so its precise meaning is uncertain.
- **Relevance to this FYP:** This paper is the closest published precedent for studying quantization through a jailbreak-compliance lens, and its central lesson disciplines how the FYP may cite it: quantization's safety effect is **attack-type-dependent** (direct vs transferred; simple vs optimised), which means the FYP's null-safety-at-NF4 headline — measured under direct requests only — is correctly scoped and must not be generalized to adversarial settings (the report already flags engaging these attack-dependent effects as the highest-leverage future-work extension; verified in `scripts/build_fyp_report_v5.js` line 1038 this session). Methodologically, its three-scorer ASR design and the authors' own judge-bias caveat (186–196) externally support the FYP's classifier-primary-plus-cross-checks scoring architecture, and the within-table scorer divergence (Human 100 vs judge-full-compliance 23.2 for AutoDAN, 274 vs 300) illustrates exactly why single-scorer ASR claims need sensitivity checks like the FYP's judge-vs-regex validation. Boundaries when citing: Vicuna 13B v1.5 (a 13B model) vs the FYP's five pairs of ≤7.2B instruct models; offline AWQ/GPTQ checkpoints vs the FYP's on-the-fly BitsAndBytes NF4/INT8 loading; a jailbreak-optimisation threat model vs the FYP's direct-request protocol; and no capability metric is co-reported, whereas the FYP's contribution is the capability-anchored matched-pair design. Its input-side severity stratification (queries graded 1–8 before any model call) suggests a feasible FYP extension — stratifying HarmBench ASR by prompt severity — but that is my inference, not something the paper does beyond its own dataset.
